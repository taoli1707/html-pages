What does a sound engineer do, and which numbers decide the result at each stage from the microphone to the listener?

Written 2026-10-12 · Main path: about 25 minutes · Complete page: about 54 minutes · You need to know: what a voltage is, and how to read a logarithm with base 10

The short answer

A sound engineer moves sound through a chain of stages: microphone, preamplifier, converter, workstation, master, listener. At every stage the engineer sets a level in decibels and keeps it between the noise and the clipping point. On this page, a Shure SM58 microphone gives 1.85 mV at 94 dB SPL. A gain of 50.3 dB brings this to −18 dBFS in the converter. A compressor (threshold −20 dBFS, ratio 4:1) reduces a 24 dB range of levels to 13.5 dB. Spotify plays the finished master at −14 LUFS, whatever its loudness was.

Assumptions

The request named no situation, so the page follows one example. One voice is recorded at home with a Shure SM58 microphone into a Focusrite Scarlett 2i2 (4th generation) audio interface. The recording uses 48 kHz and 24 bit, and the result goes to a streaming service. The levels of the voice are example values. Live sound and film sound use the same ideas (see "Words that you will hear"). The limits of the page are in section 13.

Part A: sound as a signal, and its units

The short answer for part A

Sound is a change of air pressure, in pascals (Pa). The engineer states every level in dB: 20 times the logarithm of a ratio to a reference value. The reference gives the number its meaning. Sound in air uses 20 µPa (dB SPL). A voltage uses 1 V (dBV) or 0.7746 V (dBu), and digital audio uses the largest sample (dBFS). 1 Pa is 94 dB SPL, a doubling adds 6 dB, and ten times adds 20 dB.

1. One example: the same voice in four units

The whole page follows one signal chain: the sequence of devices that a sound passes from the source to the listener. Each stage gives the sound a new physical form and needs its own unit.

Worked example: one voice, four units
  1. A singer produces a sound pressure of 1 Pa at the microphone (example value). 1 Pa is the standard test pressure of microphone data sheets.
  2. The sound pressure level is the pressure in decibels, written dB SPL. A decibel (dB) is 20 times the logarithm of a ratio: 20 × log10(1 ÷ 0.00002) = 93.98, rounded to 94 dB SPL. The reference pressure is 20 µPa, the quietest sound that a healthy ear hears (Wikipedia, Sound pressure).
  3. A second singer produces 2 Pa: 20 × log10(2 ÷ 0.00002) = 100.00 dB SPL. The doubling added 6.02 dB, not 94 dB. The logarithm of a product is a sum, so a doubling adds 20 × log10(2) = 6.02 dB.
  4. The Shure SM58 gives 1.85 mV at 1 Pa, which Shure states as −54.5 dBV per pascal. The unit dBV is the decibel with a reference of 1 V. 20 × log10(0.00185 ÷ 1) = −54.7 dBV; Shure rounds both values, and they differ by 0.2 dB.
  5. Professional equipment states voltages in dBu, the decibel with a reference of 0.7746 V. A dBu value is 2.2 dB higher than the dBV value, so the microphone gives −52.3 dBu. 20 × log10(1 ÷ 0.7746) = 2.2 dB. The reference is the voltage of 1 mW in 600 Ω (Wikipedia, Line level).
  6. In the converter, the same voice sits at −18 dBFS. The unit dBFS is the decibel relative to full scale, the largest possible sample value. 0 dBFS is the top, and every normal signal is negative. A digital format has a largest number, so every digital level is a distance below it.

Result: 94 dB SPL in the air, −54.5 dBV or −52.3 dBu at the cable, −18 dBFS in the file. The sound did not change; the reference changed.

2. The rule: a decibel is a ratio, and the reference gives it a meaning

For a pressure or a voltage, the level is L = 20 × log10(x ÷ xref). For a power, the level is L = 10 × log10(P ÷ Pref), because power grows with the square of the voltage. The reference value xref is the fixed value that the ratio compares with, and the suffix of the unit names it. A doubling of voltage is +6.02 dB, and ten times is +20 dB (Wikipedia, Decibel). A doubling of power is +3.01 dB.

A common wrong idea

Wrong: "0 dB means silence, and a negative decibel value means no signal."

Correct: 0 dB means "equal to the reference". 0 dB SPL is the quietest audible sound, and 0 dBFS is the loudest value that a digital file can hold.

Why the wrong idea fails: the decibel has no zero of its own; its zero is the reference (Table 1).

Part B: capture, from the microphone to the converter

The short answer for part B

The SM58 gives 1.85 mV at 94 dB SPL. A gain of 50.3 dB puts the average voice at −18 dBFS and the peaks at −6 dBFS. A peak 18 dB above the average reaches 0 dBFS and clips. A very calm room at 30 dB SPL gives −82 dBFS, above the preamplifier noise (−92.7 dBFS) and the 16-bit quantization noise (−98.1 dBFS). The room sets the noise floor.

4. One example: 50.3 dB of gain puts the voice at −18 dBFS, and the room sets the noise floor

Three devices capture the voice. The microphone converts air pressure into voltage. The preamplifier (from here: preamp) amplifies this voltage, and its gain is the amplification in dB. The analog-to-digital converter (ADC) measures the voltage 48,000 times per second and writes each measurement as a number.

Worked example: the gain for the SM58 into the Scarlett 2i2
  1. The microphone sensitivity is the output voltage at 1 Pa, in mV per pascal or dBV per pascal. The SM58 has −54.5 dBV per pascal, which is −52.3 dBu (part A). The SM58 is a dynamic microphone: a coil moves in a magnetic field and makes the voltage without a power supply.
  2. The Scarlett 2i2 data sheet states three values for the microphone input. The maximum input level is +16 dBu, the gain range 69 dB, and the equivalent input noise −127 dBu. The maximum input level fills the converter at the lowest gain, so +16 dBu gives 0 dBFS: dBFS = dBu − 16.
  3. The target for the average voice is −18 dBFS, which is 16 − 18 = −2 dBu, or 0.615 V. Sound On Sound recommends −18 to −10 dBFS on the workstation meter; −18 dBFS is also the European alignment level (EBU R68).
  4. The gain is the target minus the microphone level: −2 − (−52.3) = 50.3 dB, within the range of 69 dB. Gain adds in decibels.
  5. Peaks 12 dB above the average (106 dB SPL) arrive at −6 dBFS. A peak 18 dB above arrives at 0 dBFS. A peak 20 dB above would need +2 dBFS, which does not exist: the converter writes its maximum value for the whole peak. Clipping is this cut, a distortion that no later stage removes. A decibel change in the air is the same change at every later stage, because every stage multiplies by a constant.
  6. Below the voice lies the noise. A very calm room has 20 to 30 dB SPL (Wikipedia, Sound pressure). At 30 dB SPL it is 64 dB below the voice: −82 dBFS. The preamp noise, −127 dBu + 50.3 dB = −76.7 dBu, is −92.7 dBFS. The noise floor is the highest noise: the room. The equivalent input noise (EIN) is the preamp noise stated at its input, so it passes the same gain.
  7. This choice is gain staging: the gain at each stage is set so that the signal stays above the noise floor and below clipping everywhere.

Result: the voice occupies −18 dBFS (average) to −6 dBFS (peaks). The 6 dB above the peaks is the headroom: the distance to the maximum level. The noise floor is the room at −82 dBFS.

The rule of capture: the noise floor is the highest noise in the chain, here the room. A quieter room, a closer microphone, or a louder source helps more than a better converter.

A common wrong idea

Wrong: "Record as loud as possible, with peaks close to 0 dBFS, to use all the bits."

Correct: with 24 bits, peaks at −6 dBFS or even −12 dBFS lose nothing audible, because the quantization noise lies at −116 dBFS or lower.

Why the wrong idea fails: the idea comes from 16-bit converters and tape, where the noise of the medium was close to the signal.

The second microphone type is the condenser microphone. A thin charged plate moves in front of a fixed plate. The microphone needs a power supply for the charge and for a small amplifier. The comparison shows what changes.

Dynamic microphone: Shure SM58

Sensitivity −54.5 dBV per pascal (1.85 mV). Needs no power.

Gain for −18 dBFS: 50.3 dB. The preamp noise lands at −92.7 dBFS.

Condenser microphone: Audio-Technica AT2020

Sensitivity −37 dBV per pascal (14.1 mV), 17.5 dB more. Needs 48 V phantom power from the interface.

Gain for −18 dBFS: 32.8 dB. The preamp noise lands at −110.2 dBFS. The microphone itself has a self-noise of 20 dB SPL, which lands at −92 dBFS.

The principle: a more sensitive microphone needs less gain, so the preamp noise matters less. But the room at −82 dBFS stays the noise floor with both microphones. Sources: Audio-Technica AT2020 specifications; Wikipedia, Microphone.

The converter adds two numbers to the chain. The sample rate is the number of measurements per second: 48,000 per second (48 kHz) in the example. The bit depth is the number of bits in each measurement: 24 bits give 16,777,216 possible values. Each extra bit halves the step between values and lowers the quantization noise by 6.02 dB.

An ideal converter with b bits has a noise of 1.76 + 6.02 × b dB below full scale. This is 98.1 dB for 16 bits and 146.3 dB for 24 bits (Wikipedia, Audio bit depth). A real converter does not reach the 24-bit value: the Scarlett 2i2 has a measured range of 116 dB between its noise and its maximum.

The chart puts every level of the example on the dBFS scale. The signal sits at the top, and four noises sit at the bottom. The highest noise is the room.

The sample rate sets the highest frequency that a recording can hold. The frequency of a sound is its number of pressure cycles per second, in hertz (Hz). Hearing covers 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (Wikipedia, Hearing range). The Nyquist frequency is half the sample rate. The sampling theorem states that a signal is recorded completely if it contains no frequency above the Nyquist frequency (Wikipedia, Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem). With 48 kHz the Nyquist frequency is 24 kHz, above the hearing range of 20 kHz, and with 44.1 kHz it is 22.05 kHz.

A frequency above the Nyquist frequency does not disappear: the converter records it as a wrong, lower frequency, an error called aliasing. So every converter removes such frequencies with a filter before it samples. The Audio Engineering Society recommends 48 kHz for most work (Wikipedia, Sampling).

A higher bit depth helps the engineer, not the listener. During recording and processing, the signal is scaled many times, and a 24-bit file keeps 146 dB of theoretical range for this. The final file for the listener needs only the range of a listening room. Part D gives the numbers for delivery.

The converter also adds a delay. Latency is the time between the entry of a signal into a system and its exit (Wikipedia, Latency). The computer processes samples in blocks. One block of 256 samples at 48 kHz takes 256 ÷ 48,000 = 5.33 ms, and a block of 64 samples takes 1.33 ms. The signal passes a block on the way in and a block on the way out, plus the converters. So the singer hears their own voice later than they produce it.

Wikipedia gives typical latencies of 0.5 to 10 ms for digital audio devices, and up to 30 ms for some designs. A smaller block lowers the delay but gives the computer less time per block.

Part C: processing in the workstation

The short answer for part C

In the workstation the engineer shapes each track with a compressor and an equalizer, and then mixes the tracks. A compressor reduces the level above a threshold. With a threshold of −20 dBFS and a ratio of 4:1, a peak at −6 dBFS leaves at −16.5 dBFS. The range of the vocal shrinks from 24 dB to 13.5 dB. A make-up gain of +10 dB then raises the quiet syllables by 10 dB, and the peaks end at −6.5 dBFS.

7. One example: the compressor shrinks a 24 dB range to 13.5 dB

The digital audio workstation (DAW) holds the tracks and runs the processors. The engineer prepares each track for the mix: the sum of all tracks, each with its own level and position between left and right. The vocal has peaks at −6 dBFS and quiet syllables at −30 dBFS (example values). Its dynamic range, the distance between its quietest and its loudest level, is 24 dB. In the mix, the quiet syllables disappear behind the instruments. A compressor reduces the range: it lowers the level by an amount that depends on the level itself.

Worked example: the vocal through the compressor
  1. The threshold is the level above which the compressor acts: −20 dBFS here. A level below the threshold passes unchanged. The compressor should touch only the loud parts, so the threshold lies between −30 and −6.
  2. The compression ratio compares a rise of the input above the threshold with the resulting rise of the output: 4:1 here. If the input rises 4 dB above the threshold, the output rises 1 dB (Wikipedia, Dynamic range compression).
  3. The peak at −6 dBFS is 14 dB above the threshold. The output is 14 ÷ 4 = 3.5 dB above the threshold: −20 + 3.5 = −16.5 dBFS. The compressor reduced the gain by 10.5 dB at this moment. Output = threshold + (input − threshold) ÷ ratio. Gain reduction = input − output.
  4. The quiet syllable at −30 dBFS is below the threshold and leaves at −30 dBFS. The new range is −16.5 − (−30) = 13.5 dB, down from 24 dB. Only the part above the threshold shrinks.
  5. The compressor needs time. The attack time (10 ms here) is the time to apply the reduction after the input crosses the threshold. The release time (100 ms here) is the time to remove it. An instant reduction would cut the start of each syllable. Both are example values.
  6. After the compressor, the whole track is quieter. The make-up gain is a fixed gain after the compressor: +10 dB here. The peaks now sit at −16.5 + 10 = −6.5 dBFS, and the quiet syllables at −30 + 10 = −20 dBFS. A compressor only reduces (Wikipedia, Dynamic range compression).

Result: the peaks moved from −6 to −6.5 dBFS (0.5 dB down), the quiet syllables from −30 to −20 dBFS (10 dB up). The listener hears the quiet syllables, and the peaks are where they were.

A common wrong idea

Wrong: "A compressor makes a track louder."

Correct: a compressor only makes the loud parts quieter. The make-up gain after it makes the whole track louder, so the quiet parts come up.

Why the wrong idea fails: most compressors apply the make-up gain inside the same device, so the user credits the compression.

An equalizer (EQ) changes the level of some frequencies and leaves the others. The most flexible kind is the parametric equalizer, which has one or more bands with three settings each (Wikipedia, Audio equalization). The center frequency is the frequency at which the band acts most, in Hz. The gain is the change at the center, in dB: positive for a boost, negative for a cut. The Q factor is the center frequency divided by the bandwidth of the band, so a higher Q is a narrower band. A high-pass filter passes the frequencies above its cut-off frequency and removes the frequencies below it.

The example vocal gets three bands. A high-pass filter at 80 Hz removes low rumble. A cut of −3 dB at 300 Hz with Q 1.4 reduces the lower middle frequencies. A boost of +2 dB at 4 kHz with Q 1 raises the upper middle frequencies. The bandwidth of the cut is 300 ÷ 1.4 = 214 Hz, and the bandwidth of the boost is 4,000 ÷ 1 = 4,000 Hz. All three settings are example values, chosen for the sound of the voice, which this page cannot measure.

The Q factor needs one caution. The general definition is Q = center frequency ÷ bandwidth. The bandwidth is the distance between the two frequencies at which the effect has fallen to half (Wikipedia, Q factor). For a cut of 3 dB, "half" has several definitions, and equalizer makers choose different ones. So two equalizers with the setting Q 1.4 at 300 Hz can differ a little in width. The engineer sets Q by ear and reads the curve on the screen.

The mix adds the tracks. Levels add as voltages, not as decibels. Two equal signals in phase add to a signal 6.02 dB louder. So a mix of many tracks reaches 0 dBFS sooner than any one track. The rule of the mix is the rule of part B again. Lower the tracks to keep the sum below clipping, and keep headroom for the master.

The attack time and the release time decide how the compressor follows the signal. A long attack of 30 ms or more lets the start of a drum hit or a consonant pass at full level. A short attack of 1 ms catches it. A short release lets the level return quickly. When the release is shorter than one cycle of a low note, the compressor follows the wave itself and distorts it.

Many compressors offer an automatic setting that changes attack and release with the signal. The values 10 ms and 100 ms of the example are a judgment of this page, not a measurement.

The compressor formula of this page has a hard knee: the curve bends sharply at the threshold. Many compressors use a soft knee. The reduction then starts a few dB below the threshold and reaches the full ratio a few dB above it (Wikipedia, Dynamic range compression).

Part D: delivery, from the master to the listener

The short answer for part D

The loudness of a whole track is measured in LUFS, and Spotify plays every track at −14 LUFS in its normal setting. A loud master at −9 LUFS is turned down by 5 dB. A quiet master at −20 LUFS is turned up only as far as its true peak allows, to −16 LUFS. So a loud master gains nothing on a normalized service, and it loses dynamic range.

10. One example: three masters of one song on Spotify

Mastering is the last step of production: the engineer prepares the master, the file from which all copies are made (Wikipedia, Mastering). Services measure the master as loudness: the perceived level of a track, measured by a fixed algorithm (the standard ITU-R BS.1770). The unit is LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale); a gain of 1 dB raises the loudness by 1 LUFS. The value for a whole track is the integrated loudness. The master also has a true peak: the highest level between the samples, in dBTP (EBU R 128). Loudness normalization is the adjustment of the playback gain by a service, so that every track plays at its target: −14 LUFS at Spotify.

Worked example: masters A, B, and C on Spotify at the normal setting
  1. Master A is loud: −9 LUFS, true peak −0.1 dBTP. Master B is normal: −14 LUFS, true peak −1 dBTP. Master C is quiet: −20 LUFS, true peak −5 dBTP. All three are example values. Master A also lost its peaks in the process that made it loud.
  2. Spotify plays master A at −14 LUFS, so it applies −14 − (−9) = −5 dB. The peak falls to −5.1 dBTP. Spotify states that negative gain is applied to louder masters until the loudness is −14 LUFS.
  3. Master B needs 0 dB, because its loudness already equals the target. It plays as it was mastered.
  4. Master C would need +6 dB. But +6 dB would move its true peak from −5 to +1 dBTP, above full scale. Spotify keeps 1 dB of headroom, so the gain is −1 − (−5) = +4 dB, and master C plays at −16 LUFS. Spotify states this case with these numbers. The 1 dB of headroom protects the lossy encoding.
  5. Result: masters A and B play at the same level, and master C plays 2 dB quieter. The extra 5 dB of master A bought nothing, and master A has the smallest dynamic range. Normalization reads one number, the integrated loudness.

The rule of delivery: every decibel above the target is removed at playback. Every decibel below the target is added only while the true peak allows. So the mastering engineer delivers two numbers: an integrated loudness near the target, and a true peak at or below −1 dBTP.

A common wrong idea

Wrong: "A louder master sounds louder for the listener on a streaming service, so a loud master competes better."

Correct: on a normalized service, a master at −9 LUFS and a master at −14 LUFS play at the same loudness. The louder master is turned down by 5 dB, and it has lost dynamic range.

Why the wrong idea fails: it was true on the compact disc, which has no normalization. The listener never hears the extra 5 dB.

11. More detail: the targets, the loudness meter, and the final format

A master above the target paid for its loudness with dynamic range. A mix that already peaks near 0 dBFS can become louder only through a limiter. A limiter is a compressor with a very high ratio that holds every peak below a ceiling. The limiter removes the peaks, and the normalization then removes the loudness. The loudness war of the years 1990 to 2010 was the race for a louder master on the compact disc (Wikipedia, Loudness war). The compact disc has no normalization, so on a normalized service the race has no prize.

How to read this table: a master for broadcast must be 9 dB quieter than a master for Spotify. Both must keep the true peak below −1 dBTP. The "true peak limit" of Spotify is the headroom rule for positive gain, not a rejection rule for uploads.

ITU-R BS.1770 defines the meter in four steps. First, a filter called K-weighting changes the signal like the head and the ear. It has a high-frequency shelf for the effect of the head, modelled as a rigid sphere, and a high-pass filter. Second, the meter computes the mean square of each channel. Third, it sums the channels with weights: larger weights for the surround channels, and no low-frequency effects channel.

Fourth, it measures in blocks of 400 ms that overlap by 75%, and it ignores blocks below two gates. The absolute gate at −70 LUFS removes silence. The relative gate, 10 dB below the level after the first gate, removes quiet passages. The integrated loudness is the average of the remaining blocks. The standard names the unit LKFS (loudness, K-weighted, relative to full scale). EBU R 128 named the same unit LUFS, and the two are identical (Wikipedia, LUFS).

The true peak comes from the same standard. The meter raises the sample rate of the signal for the measurement, so it sees the peaks that lie between two samples. A sample peak meter in a workstation can show −0.1 dBFS while the true peak is above 0 dBTP.

The final format closes the chain. The compact disc takes 16 bits at 44.1 kHz. When the engineer reduces a 24-bit master to 16 bits, the last 8 bits are cut. Dither is a small noise added before this cut. It turns the error of the cut into a constant, quiet noise instead of a distortion that depends on the signal (Wikipedia, Dither).

Streaming services receive the master as a file and convert it themselves. Apple describes its process: the sample rate conversion writes a 32-bit floating point file, so that no clipping occurs in the conversion (Apple Digital Masters). Apple also states that 24-bit recording and sample rates of 48 to 192 kHz have become standard in production.

For the listener, 16 bits give 98 dB of range. This is more than the 64 dB between the voice and the room noise of part B, and 44.1 kHz covers the hearing range.

Where the explanation stops
  • The levels of the voice, the vocal track, the processor settings, and the three masters are example values. A singer close to the microphone can be much louder: Wikipedia gives 135 dB SPL at 1 inch for the loudest voice. So the engineer reads the real peaks on the meter.
  • The room noise of 30 dB SPL is the Wikipedia value for a very calm room, without a citation there. The noise levels of part B are calculated, not measured.
  • The relation dBFS = dBu − 16 holds for the Scarlett 2i2 at minimum gain. Other interfaces differ, and European broadcast equipment uses +18 dBu for 0 dBFS (EBU R68).
  • The compressor formula has a hard knee, and the equalizer bandwidth follows one definition. Real devices differ near the threshold and at the edges of a band.
  • Spotify states that some third-party devices do not use loudness normalization, and listeners can switch it off. On such a device, master A does play 5 dB louder than master B. The targets of other services were not verified.
  • The page omits reverberation, delay, pitch correction, stereo placement, live sound, and film sound; some of their words are in the next section.

14. Words that you will hear

These words belong to the work of a sound engineer, but not to the level arithmetic above. Each entry gives the meaning and the connection to the main idea.

Phantom power. A direct voltage of 48 V that the interface sends over the microphone cable to a condenser microphone. The IEC 61938 standard also defines 24 V and 12 V versions (Wikipedia, Phantom power). The AT2020 of part B needs it, and the SM58 does not.

Polar pattern, cardioid. The polar pattern is the sensitivity of a microphone by direction. A cardioid pattern is most sensitive at the front and least at the back. Both the SM58 and the AT2020 are cardioid, so they take less room sound than a microphone with equal sensitivity in all directions.

Proximity effect. A directional microphone a few centimetres from the source gives more bass (Wikipedia, Microphone). The engineer corrects it with the high-pass filter of part C.

3-to-1 rule and comb filtering. With two microphones, the distance between the microphones should be at least three times the distance from each microphone to its source (Wikipedia, Microphone practice). Otherwise the same sound arrives at both microphones with a delay, and the mix cancels some frequencies: comb filtering.

Balanced audio, XLR. The microphone cable carries the signal on two wires with opposite polarity, and the input subtracts them. Noise that enters both wires equally cancels (Wikipedia, Balanced audio). This is why the 50.3 dB of gain in part B does not also amplify the noise that the cable collects. XLR is the three-pin connector of this cable.

Audio feedback, Larsen effect. A loudspeaker feeds its sound back into the microphone. When the gain around this loop reaches 1, the system oscillates at one frequency (Wikipedia, Audio feedback). In a live situation it limits the gain of part B. The engineer lowers the gain or cuts the frequency with the equalizer.

Reverberation time, T60. The time in which the sound in a room falls by 60 dB after the source stops (Wikipedia, Reverberation). A long time adds room sound to the recording, which the noise floor of part B does not show but the listener hears.

Bus, send. In the mix, a bus is a sum of several tracks that is processed as one signal. A send copies a part of a track to a bus, for example to a shared reverberation effect.

Plug-in. The equalizer and the compressor of part C run as software inside the workstation. Each such processor is a plug-in.

Aliasing, anti-aliasing filter. See part B, section 5. A frequency above the Nyquist frequency is recorded as a wrong frequency, and the filter before the converter removes it.

Floating point, 32-bit float. A workstation calculates with floating point numbers, which have no fixed maximum. So an internal level above 0 dBFS does not clip until the signal reaches a converter or an integer file. The headroom rule of part C still applies at the output.

Loudness range (LRA). A number from EBU R 128 that describes how much the loudness of a programme varies over time, in LU. R 128 sets no maximum for it. It is the dynamic range of part C, measured with the loudness meter of part D.

Lossy codec. A format such as AAC or MP3 that removes parts of the signal that a model of hearing predicts to be inaudible. Spotify keeps 1 dB of headroom in part D because such an encoder can raise the true peak.

15. Recap, glossary, and sources

Recap
  1. A decibel is 20 times the logarithm of a ratio, and the reference gives it a meaning. The references: 20 µPa (dB SPL), 1 V (dBV), 0.7746 V (dBu), the largest sample (dBFS).
  2. Capture is gain staging: 50.3 dB of gain puts the SM58 voice at −18 dBFS with peaks at −6 dBFS. The room at −82 dBFS is the noise floor, above the preamp and the quantization noise.
  3. A compressor lowers the level above a threshold by the ratio: with −20 dBFS and 4:1, a −6 dBFS peak leaves at −16.5 dBFS. Make-up gain then raises the quiet parts by 10 dB.
  4. A parametric equalizer changes one band by center frequency, gain, and Q. The mix adds the tracks as voltages, so it needs headroom.
  5. Delivery is measured in LUFS. Spotify plays every master at −14 LUFS and turns a −9 LUFS master down by 5 dB. EBU R 128 uses −23 LUFS and −1 dBTP. A loud master gains nothing.

Glossary

analog-to-digital converter (ADC)
The device that measures the voltage at the sample rate and writes each measurement as a number with the bit depth.
attack time
The time a compressor takes to apply its gain reduction after the input crosses the threshold.
bit depth
The number of bits in each sample; 24 bits give 16,777,216 values and a theoretical noise of 146.3 dB below full scale.
center frequency
The frequency at which a band of a parametric equalizer acts most.
clipping
The cut of a signal at the maximum value of a device, which adds distortion that no later stage removes.
compression ratio
The comparison between a rise of the input above the threshold and the resulting rise of the output of a compressor, for example 4:1.
compressor
A processor that lowers the level of a signal by an amount that depends on the level itself. Its settings are threshold, ratio, attack time, and release time.
condenser microphone
A microphone with a thin charged plate in front of a fixed plate; it needs a power supply such as phantom power.
dBFS
Decibels relative to full scale, the largest sample value of a digital system; 0 dBFS is the top, and every normal signal is negative.
dBu
Decibels relative to 0.7746 V, the unit of professional equipment data sheets; 2.2 dB above the dBV value.
dBV
Decibels relative to 1 V, the unit of microphone sensitivity.
decibel (dB)
A logarithmic measure of a ratio: 20 × log10(x ÷ xref) for pressure and voltage, 10 × log10 for power.
dither
A small noise added before a reduction of the bit depth, which turns the rounding error into a constant quiet noise.
dynamic microphone
A microphone in which a coil moves in a magnetic field and produces the voltage without a power supply.
dynamic range
The distance in dB between the quietest usable level and the loudest level of a signal or a device.
equalizer (EQ)
A processor that changes the level of some frequencies and leaves the others.
frequency
The number of pressure cycles per second, in hertz (Hz).
gain
An amplification stated in dB; gains add along the chain, and a loss is a negative gain.
gain staging
The choice of the gain at each stage, so that the signal stays above the noise floor and below the clipping point everywhere.
headroom
The distance in dB between the normal working level and the maximum level before distortion.
high-pass filter
A filter that passes the frequencies above its cut-off frequency and removes the frequencies below it.
integrated loudness
The loudness of a whole track, in LUFS, measured with the gated average of ITU-R BS.1770.
latency
The time between the entry of a signal into a system and its exit; one block of 256 samples at 48 kHz is 5.33 ms.
limiter
A compressor with a very high ratio that holds every peak below a ceiling.
loudness
The perceived level of a whole track, measured by the algorithm of ITU-R BS.1770.
loudness normalization
The adjustment of the playback gain by a service, so that every track plays at the same integrated loudness.
LUFS
Loudness units relative to full scale, the unit of loudness; identical to LKFS of ITU-R BS.1770.
make-up gain
A fixed gain after a compressor that raises the whole track.
mastering
The last step of production, which prepares the master file from the final mix.
microphone sensitivity
The output voltage of a microphone at 1 Pa (94 dB SPL), in mV per pascal or dBV per pascal.
mix
The sum of all tracks, each with its own level and its own position between left and right.
noise floor
The highest noise in a chain, below which no signal is usable.
Nyquist frequency
Half the sample rate, the highest frequency that a sampled signal can hold.
parametric equalizer
An equalizer with bands that each have a center frequency, a gain, and a Q factor.
preamplifier (preamp)
The amplifier that raises the small microphone voltage to a usable level.
Q factor
The center frequency of a band divided by its bandwidth; a higher Q is a narrower band.
reference value
The fixed value with which a decibel compares; the suffix of the unit names it.
release time
The time a compressor takes to remove its gain reduction after the input falls below the threshold.
sample rate
The number of measurements per second of a converter, for example 48,000 per second.
signal chain
The sequence of devices that a sound passes from the source to the listener.
sound pressure level (dB SPL)
The sound pressure in decibels relative to 20 µPa; 1 Pa is 94 dB SPL.
threshold
The level above which a compressor acts.
true peak
The highest level of the signal between the samples, in dBTP, found with a raised sample rate in the meter.
