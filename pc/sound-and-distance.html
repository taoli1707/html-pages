How does distance change the volume of a sound, and what does a fan rated 23.3 dB(A) sound like at your desk?

Written 2026-10-12 · Reading time: about 30 minutes · You need to know: what a logarithm is. The page explains every acoustic term.

The short answer

In open air, the sound level of a small source falls by 6 dB each time the distance doubles. It rises by 6 dB each time the distance halves. The formula is L2 = L1 − 20 × log10(d2 ÷ d1). A fan rated 23.3 dB(A) at 1 m gives 26.4 dB(A) at 0.7 m, 29.3 dB(A) at 0.5 m, and 17.3 dB(A) at 2 m.

A change of 6 dB is not "half as loud" or "twice as loud". The ear needs about 10 dB for a doubling of perceived loudness. So the step from 1 m to 0.5 m sounds about 1.5 times as loud, not twice as loud.

In a room, reflections add to the direct sound. In a room of 40 m³ with a reverberation time of 0.5 s, the level stops falling beyond about 0.5 m from the source. At 2 m the same fan then gives about 29 dB(A), not 17 dB(A). So at a desk, the room matters more than the distance.

Both numbers that you compared were measured at 1 m: HWBusters places the case 1 m from the microphone. The distance behind the be quiet! datasheet value is not published, so this page assumes 1 m for it as well.

1. One example: 23.3 dB(A) at 1 m becomes 26.4 dB(A) at 0.7 m

The be quiet! datasheet rates the Light Wings 140 PWM fan at 23.3 dB(A) at full speed. Sound meters report sound pressure: the small, fast change of air pressure that a sound adds to the normal air pressure, measured in pascals (Pa). The datasheet does not give the pressure. It gives the sound pressure level (short form: SPL): the sound pressure on a logarithmic scale in decibels (dB). The zero point is 20 µPa, and the formula is SPL = 20 × log10(p ÷ 20 µPa).

The "(A)" in dB(A) means that the meter applied a filter that imitates the ear. Section 5 explains the filter.

A level in decibels is only complete with a distance, because the same fan gives a different number at every distance. The worked example moves the datasheet value from 1 m to 0.7 m, the distance from a case on the desk to your ear.

Worked example: the fan at 0.7 m
  1. Write the known values: L1 = 23.3 dB(A) at d1 = 1 m. The new distance is d2 = 0.7 m. The datasheet value is the start point. Without a distance for it, no calculation is possible.
  2. Calculate the ratio of the distances: d2 ÷ d1 = 0.7 ÷ 1 = 0.7. Only the ratio matters. A move from 2 m to 1.4 m gives the same change as a move from 1 m to 0.7 m.
  3. Take the logarithm: log10(0.7) = −0.1549. The decibel scale is logarithmic, so a ratio of pressures becomes a difference of levels.
  4. Multiply by 20: 20 × (−0.1549) = −3.10 dB. The factor is 20 because the level is defined from the pressure, and the pressure falls in proportion to 1 ÷ distance. Section 2 shows why.
  5. Subtract: L2 = 23.3 − (−3.10) = 26.4 dB(A). The distance became smaller, so the logarithm is negative, and the level rises.

Result: at 0.7 m, the fan gives 26.4 dB(A). At 0.5 m it gives 23.3 + 6.0 = 29.3 dB(A). At 2 m it gives 23.3 − 6.0 = 17.3 dB(A).

The same steps apply to the case values from HWBusters. HWBusters measures in a hemi-anechoic chamber with the case 1 m from the microphone. The chamber has a noise floor close to 6 dB(A).

The Shadow Base 800 FX with its fans at 50% gave 12.6 dB(A) at 1 m. At 0.7 m this becomes 12.6 + 3.1 = 15.7 dB(A). At 1.5 m, with the case on the floor, it becomes 12.6 − 3.5 = 9.1 dB(A). The Dark Base Pro 901 at 50%: 26.4 dB(A) at 1 m, 29.5 at 0.7 m, and 22.9 at 1.5 m.

2. The rule: the same sound power spreads over a sphere that grows with the square of the distance

A fan sends out a fixed amount of sound power, measured in watts. This power spreads outward in all directions. At a distance r, it passes through the surface of a sphere with radius r, which is 4 × π × r².

At 2 m the sphere has four times the area of the sphere at 1 m. At 3 m it has nine times the area.

The power per square metre is the sound intensity: the sound power that passes through one square metre. Its unit is W per m². The 1 µW source in Figure 1 gives 1 µW ÷ 12.566 m² = 7.96 × 10⁻⁸ W per m² at 1 m. At 2 m it gives one quarter of that. This is the inverse square law: the intensity falls in proportion to 1 ÷ r².

The law holds for a point source: a source that is small compared with the distance, so that the sound spreads equally in all directions. A fan or a PC case is a point source for a listener a few metres away.

Two more conditions apply. The listener must be in the far field: the region where pressure and distance follow the simple law. It begins at about one wavelength and about three source sizes from the source. Closer, in the near field, no simple law applies. The listener must also be in a free field: a space with no reflections, such as open air or an anechoic chamber. Section 3 shows what a room changes.

Now the step from intensity to pressure. The intensity is proportional to the square of the sound pressure. So when the intensity falls to one quarter, the pressure falls to one half. The level uses 20 × log10 of a pressure ratio, or equally 10 × log10 of an intensity ratio: 20 × log10(1 ÷ 2) = 10 × log10(1 ÷ 4) = −6.02 dB. This is the rule of 6 dB per doubling of the distance.

3. Line sources and rooms: 3 dB per doubling, or no change at all beyond the critical distance

The 6 dB rule is for a point source in a free field. Two other cases are common. The first is a line source: a source that is long compared with the distance, such as a road with steady traffic. Its sound spreads over the surface of a cylinder, not a sphere. The cylinder area grows in proportion to r, not r², so the level falls by only 3 dB per doubling. The formula becomes L2 = L1 − 10 × log10(d2 ÷ d1).

The second case is a room. Sound that reaches the walls comes back. The reflected sound fills the room almost evenly. It forms the reverberant field: the part of the sound that arrives through reflections. This part has about the same level everywhere in the room.

The direct sound still falls by 6 dB per doubling. At some distance the two are equal. This distance is the critical distance: the distance from the source at which the direct sound and the reverberant sound have the same level. Beyond it, the reverberant field dominates, and the level hardly changes with distance.

For a source that radiates equally in all directions, dc = 0.057 × √(V ÷ T60). V is the room volume in cubic metres. T60 is the reverberation time: the seconds in which the sound in the room falls by 60 dB after the source stops. For the example room: dc = 0.057 × √(40 ÷ 0.5) = 0.057 × 8.94 = 0.51 m. A more absorbent room with T60 = 0.3 s gives 0.66 m, and a bare room with T60 = 1.0 s gives 0.36 m.

Guess first

The fan gives 23.3 dB(A) at 1 m in a free field. In the example room, what is the level at 2 m from the fan: about 17, 23, or 29 dB(A)? Choose one before you continue.

Answer to the guess: about 29 dB(A). In the room, the level at 2 m is 29.4 dB(A), 12.1 dB above the free-field value of 17.3 dB(A). From 0.5 m to 2 m the free-field level falls by 12.0 dB, the room level by only 2.8 dB.

The principle: the distance rule describes the direct sound only. In a room, it matters only inside the critical distance, about half a metre in a furnished room. Beyond that, the room decides the level. A case on the floor 1.5 m away is therefore not much quieter than a case on the desk at 0.7 m. The way to a lower level at the ear is a quieter source, or more absorption in the room.

4. Loudness: the ear needs 10 dB for "twice as loud"

A level in decibels describes the air. Loudness describes the ear: it is the perceived strength of a sound, measured in sones. One sone is a 1 kHz tone at 40 dB. Stanley Stevens defined the scale in 1936.

On this scale, loudness grows as a power of the intensity with exponent 0.3. A doubling of loudness therefore needs an intensity ratio of 2 to the power of (1 ÷ 0.3) = 10.1, which is 10.0 dB. The manufacturer be quiet! states the same 10 dB rule in its own article about fan noise.

Apply this to the fan. From 1 m to 0.5 m the level rises by 6.0 dB, so the loudness rises to 152%. From 1 m to 2 m it falls by 6.0 dB, so the loudness falls to 66%. Half as loud needs 10 dB, which is a distance ratio of 10 to the power of (10 ÷ 20) = 3.16. So the fan at 3.2 m sounds half as loud as at 1 m, in a free field.

5. dB(A) and the sum of several fans: two equal fans add 3 dB

The ear is less sensitive to low and very high frequencies than to frequencies around 1 to 4 kHz. Sound meters therefore apply A-weighting: a frequency filter that reduces low and very high frequencies before the level is calculated. The standard IEC 61672 defines it. A level measured with this filter is written dB(A). The filter changes nothing about the distance rule, because the rule reduces all frequencies equally. Figure 4 shows the filter.

A fan hum at 100 Hz loses 19.1 dB in the filter. So a fan with a strong low hum can have a low dB(A) value and still be audible.

Levels of several sources do not add as numbers. Their intensities add. Two equal fans give twice the intensity, which is 10 × log10(2) = 3.0 dB more than one fan. Four equal fans give 6.0 dB more, and ten fans give 10.0 dB more, which is twice as loud. Two Light Wings 140 fans at 1 m therefore give 23.3 + 3.0 = 26.3 dB(A).

The sum of one Light Wings at 23.3 dB(A) and one Silent Wings 4 high-speed at 29.3 dB(A) is 10 × log10(10^2.33 + 10^2.93) = 30.3 dB(A). The louder source dominates: the quieter fan adds only 1.0 dB.

6. Wrong idea corrected: twice the distance is not half as loud

Common wrong idea

Wrong: "At twice the distance the sound is half as loud."

Correct: at twice the distance, the level is 6 dB lower, and the ear hears about 66% of the loudness. Half as loud needs about 10 dB, which is 3.2 times the distance in a free field. In a room, no distance beyond the critical distance gives half the loudness.

Why the wrong idea fails: three quantities halve at three different distances. The pressure halves at 2 times the distance (−6 dB), the intensity at 1.41 times (−3 dB), and the loudness at 3.16 times (−10 dB). The wrong idea mixes the pressure with the loudness.

7. Limits: near field, unknown datasheet distance, and real rooms

Limits
  • The datasheet distance is not verified. The be quiet! datasheets state 23.3 dB(A) and 29.3 dB(A) with no distance. The HWBusters distance of 1 m is verified. If be quiet! measured at another distance, every datasheet row in the tables moves by the same amount.
  • A desk distance is near the far-field limit. At 100 Hz the wavelength is 3.43 m, and three times the size of a 0.55 m case is 1.65 m. So at 0.7 m the 6 dB rule is approximate for low frequencies.
  • The room values are a model. Real rooms are not evenly reverberant. A desk, a wall behind the case, and a carpet change the level by several decibels.
  • The loudness rule is an average. The sone scale is valid above about 40 dB. The fan values on this page are below 40 dB(A). There the ear is less sensitive, and a 10 dB step changes the loudness by less than a factor of 2.
  • Air absorption is negligible here. At 20 °C and 50% humidity, air absorbs about 4.7 dB per kilometre at 1 kHz and 105 dB per kilometre at 8 kHz. At 2 m that is 0.2 dB at 8 kHz. The FHWA states that absorption matters over distances greater than 30 m.
  • The values near 6 dB(A) sit on the noise floor. The HWBusters chamber has a noise floor close to 6 dB(A), so a calculated 6.6 dB(A) at 2 m cannot be confirmed there.

8. Words that you will hear

Anechoic and hemi-anechoic chamber
A room with absorbent wedges on all surfaces (anechoic) or on all surfaces except the floor (hemi-anechoic). It approximates a free field. HWBusters uses a hemi-anechoic chamber.
Equal-loudness contours and phon
Curves, standardised in ISO 226, that give the level at each frequency that sounds as loud as a 1 kHz tone. The phon is the loudness level: a sound of 50 phon sounds as loud as a 1 kHz tone at 50 dB. The A-weighting filter is an approximation of one of these curves.
LAeq
The A-weighted level averaged over a measurement time, as an equivalent steady level. HWBusters reports its fan noise values as LAeq.
Noise floor
The lowest level that a measurement setup can report, set by the room and the microphone. The HWBusters chamber has a noise floor close to 6 dB(A).
Sound power level
The total sound power of a source on a decibel scale, with 10⁻¹² W as the zero point. It does not depend on distance. Appliance labels use it; fan datasheets use the sound pressure level at a distance instead.
Sound intensity level
The intensity on a decibel scale, with 10⁻¹² W per m² as the zero point. In a free field it equals the sound pressure level within a fraction of a decibel. So the two are used as one number.

9. Check your understanding

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. A case gives 26.4 dB(A) at 1 m in a free field. What is the level at 0.25 m?
    Show the answer

    38.4 dB(A). log10(0.25) = −0.602, times 20 gives −12.0 dB, and 26.4 − (−12.0) = 38.4. Two halvings give two steps of 6 dB. A typical wrong answer is 52.8, which doubles the decibels instead.

  2. You move from 0.7 m to 1.5 m from a case in a free field. By how much does the level fall, and how much of the loudness remains?
    Show the answer

    The ratio is 1.5 ÷ 0.7 = 2.14, log10(2.14) = 0.331, times 20 gives 6.6 dB. The loudness falls to 2^(−6.6 ÷ 10) = 0.63, so about 63% remains. A typical wrong answer says "less than half", which would need a fall of more than 10 dB.

  3. A room has a volume of 60 m³ and a reverberation time of 0.6 s. What is its critical distance, and does the 6 dB rule apply at a desk distance of 0.7 m?
    Show the answer

    dc = 0.057 × √(60 ÷ 0.6) = 0.057 × 10 = 0.57 m. The desk distance of 0.7 m is beyond it, so the reverberant field dominates and the level changes little with distance. The 6 dB rule applies only inside about 0.5 m.

  4. Three equal fans each give 20.0 dB(A) at 1 m. What is the level of all three at 1 m?
    Show the answer

    24.8 dB(A). The intensities add: 10 × log10(3) = 4.77 dB, and 20.0 + 4.8 = 24.8. A typical wrong answer is 60 dB(A), which adds the decibel values as numbers.

10. Recap, glossary, and sources

Recap
  1. For a point source in a free field, L2 = L1 − 20 × log10(d2 ÷ d1): 6 dB per doubling or halving. The datasheet fan goes from 23.3 dB(A) at 1 m to 26.4 at 0.7 m and 17.3 at 2 m.
  2. The rule comes from the sphere: the same power spreads over 4πr², the intensity falls with 1 ÷ r², and the pressure with 1 ÷ r.
  3. In a room, reflections add a constant reverberant level. Beyond the critical distance, about 0.5 m in a furnished room, the distance hardly matters.
  4. A 6 dB change is heard as a factor of 1.5, not 2. Twice as loud needs 10 dB, which is 3.2 times the distance in a free field.
  5. Two equal fans add 3 dB. Both HWBusters values are measured at 1 m; the datasheet distance is not published.

Glossary

A-weighting
A frequency filter, defined in IEC 61672, that reduces low and very high frequencies before the level is calculated, to imitate the ear. The result is written dB(A).
Critical distance
The distance from a source in a room at which the direct sound and the reverberant sound have the same level. Formula: 0.057 × √(V ÷ T60).
Far field
The region farther than about one wavelength and three source sizes from the source, where the level follows the simple distance rule. Closer is the near field.
Free field
A space with no reflections, such as open air or an anechoic chamber.
Inverse square law
The intensity of a point source falls in proportion to 1 ÷ r², because the sound power spreads over a sphere of area 4πr².
Line source
A source that is long compared with the distance. Its level falls by 3 dB per doubling of the distance.
Loudness
The perceived strength of a sound, measured in sones. One sone is a 1 kHz tone at 40 dB. Each 10 dB doubles the loudness.
Point source
A source that is small compared with the distance, so that the sound spreads equally in all directions.
Reverberant field
The part of the sound in a room that arrives through reflections and has about the same level everywhere.
Sound intensity
The sound power that passes through one square metre, in W per m².
Sound pressure
The small, fast change of air pressure that a sound adds to the normal air pressure, in pascals.
Sound pressure level
The sound pressure on a logarithmic scale in decibels, with 20 µPa as the zero point: 20 × log10(p ÷ 20 µPa).
