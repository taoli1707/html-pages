What is first-principles reasoning, and how do you use it in engineering?

Written 2026-10-08 · Reading time: about 24 minutes · You need to know: basic arithmetic with units

The short answer

First-principles reasoning is a method. You take a claim, you find the facts under it that you can verify, and you calculate what those facts allow. The result is a limit (a number that no design can pass), not a prediction.

Example: in 2012 people said that a battery pack cost about US$600 per kilowatt-hour, and they believed that this price was permanent. The raw materials in a pack cost about $80 per kilowatt-hour. So the price could fall by a factor of up to 7.5. By 2024 the measured average price was $115 per kilowatt-hour.

The method is slow, so you use it only for a decision that matters. For everything else you copy what works, and that is correct.

Assumptions on this page
  • You asked about the method of reasoning, not about the philosophy of Aristotle. The page gives the origin of the term in one paragraph only.
  • You want to use the method at work: for estimates, for design decisions, and for the review of claims from other people.

1. One example: the material cost of a battery was $80 per kilowatt-hour, not $600

In 2012 Elon Musk described the method in an interview with Kevin Rose. The example was the price of a battery pack for an electric car. The unit of this price is the kilowatt-hour (kWh). One kilowatt-hour is the energy that a 1,000 watt device uses in one hour. A battery pack of 60 kWh at $600 per kWh costs $36,000.

The steps below follow the interview. The quotations are from the CNBC article of 2020 that reports the interview. The numbers come from Musk, so their confidence is Medium. Section 4 compares them with measured prices.

Worked example
  1. Write the claim and the belief under it. The claim: a battery pack costs $600 per kWh. The belief: this price is permanent. Musk said that people accepted the belief because of history. His words: Historically, it has cost $600 per kilowatt hour. A belief that comes from history is an analogy: the future is like the past. An analogy is a comparison, not a proof.
  2. Decompose the pack into facts that you can verify. A battery cell consists of materials: cobalt, nickel, aluminium, carbon, polymers for the separator, and a steel can. Each material has a public price on a metal exchange. A material price is a fact that you can find in public, and nobody at the company controls it. It does not depend on how batteries are made today.
  3. Calculate what the facts allow. Musk added the material prices for the materials in one kilowatt-hour of cells. His result: It's like $80 per kilowatt hour. No battery can cost less than its materials. So $80 per kWh is a limit from below for the price of the materials inside.
  4. Compare the limit with the current state. $600 ÷ $80 = 7.5. The price in 2012 was 7.5 times the material cost. The difference, $520 per kWh, was the cost of manufacturing, of the design, and of profit. A large ratio means that most of the price is not fixed by nature. So a better process can reduce the price a lot.

Result: the belief "the price is permanent" is false. The facts allow a price far below $600 per kWh. The facts do not say how to reach it. Musk said that you need clever ways to take those materials and combine them into the shape of a battery cell.

Note what the method did not do. It did not predict the price in 2024. It did not design a factory. It only showed that the belief had no basis in the facts, and it measured the size of the gap.

2. The rule: find the facts under the claim, then calculate the limit

A first principle is a fact that you accept without a further proof in the current problem. For an engineer, a first principle is often a law of physics, a measured value, a public price, or a definition. First-principles reasoning starts from these facts and builds up. The opposite method is reasoning by analogy: you copy a known solution and change it a little.

Musk described both in a talk at TED in 2013. He said that first-principles reasoning means boil things down to their fundamental truths and reason up from there, as opposed to reasoning by analogy. He also said that you must use analogy for most of your day. Otherwise you cannot work at all.

The term is old. Aristotle wrote in the Physics that we do not know a thing until we are acquainted with its primary conditions or first principles. He also wrote that the natural way is to start from what is clear to us and move to what is clear by nature. The method on this page follows that order: you start from the claim that you hear, and you move to the facts under it.

The four steps in general form:

  1. Write the claim and the belief under it. Ask: "Why do we believe this?" If the answer is "because it was always so" or "because the other team does it", the belief is an analogy.
  2. Decompose the claim into facts that you can verify. Stop when a further question has no useful answer. Stop also when the answer is a law of physics, a measurement, a public price, or a definition.
  3. Calculate the limit that the facts allow. This is often a sum, a product, or a division with units. Write every step with its unit.
  4. Compare the limit with the current state. The ratio between them is the gap. A large gap means that the current state is far from the limit, so improvement is possible. A small gap means that the current state is near the limit, so a better process will not help much.

Compare the two methods

Reasoning by analogy

You copy a known case and change it a little.

Fast. Correct when the new case is like the known case. Wrong when an important fact has changed.

First-principles reasoning

You rebuild the answer from facts that you verified.

Slow. It finds the limit, and it tests the belief. It does not give the design.

The principle after the comparison: use analogy by default, because it is cheap. Use first principles when the cost of a wrong belief is high. Use them also when someone says "that is impossible" or "that is just how it is".

3. A second example: 1 terabyte over a 1 gigabit link takes 2 hours 13 minutes, not all night

This example differs from the battery example in one feature: here you have every fact, so the confidence of the result is High. A colleague says: "Copy the 1 terabyte database to the new server tonight. It takes all night." The belief under the claim: large copies take all night. The belief comes from an old experience with an older network, so it is an analogy.

Worked example
  1. 1 terabyte is 1,000,000,000,000 bytes (1012 bytes, the SI definition). One byte is 8 bits. So the data is 8 × 1012 bits. The network speed is given in bits per second, so the data must be in bits too.
  2. The link speed is 1 gigabit per second, which is 109 bits per second. Time = 8 × 1012 ÷ 109 = 8,000 seconds. Time is the amount divided by the rate. The units confirm it: bits ÷ (bits per second) = seconds.
  3. 8,000 seconds = 2 hours 13 minutes 20 seconds. 8,000 ÷ 3,600 = 2.22 hours. The remainder, 800 seconds, is 13 minutes 20 seconds.
  4. Check the other limit: the disk. A hard disk drive reads about 150 megabytes per second (example value). 1012 ÷ (1.5 × 108) = 6,667 seconds = 1 hour 51 minutes. The copy cannot be faster than its slowest part. The network, 8,000 seconds, is slower than the disk, 6,667 seconds. So the network is the limit.

Result: the copy takes at least 2 hours 13 minutes, not all night. If the link is 10 gigabits per second, the network limit falls to 800 seconds, and the disk, at 6,667 seconds, becomes the limit.

The result is a limit, not a measurement. Protocol overhead, other traffic, and a slow receiver make the real copy slower. But the result changes the plan. You can start the copy at 18:00 and verify the result at 21:00. You do not need a night shift. Step 4 shows a general rule: when several facts each give a limit, the real limit is the slowest one.

4. The data: battery pack prices fell from above $1,100 to $115 per kilowatt-hour

BloombergNEF (from here: BNEF), a research company, publishes a yearly survey of battery pack prices. Its numbers test the battery example. The question of this section: did the price move toward the $80 limit?

Guess first

In 2024, the measured average price of a battery pack for a battery electric car was how many times the $80 material cost from 2012? Choose before you continue: 1.2 times, 2 times, or 4 times.

Answer to the guess: $97 ÷ $80 = 1.21. The price for electric car packs in 2024 was about 1.2 times the material cost that Musk stated in 2012.

The data confirm the method in two ways. First, the belief "the price is permanent" was false: the price fell from $600 to $115 per kWh, a fall of 81% relative. Second, no measured price fell below the $80 material limit. The limit held for 12 years. BNEF names the reasons for the fall: factory overcapacity, economies of scale, low metal prices, and cheaper chemistries. These are the clever ways that the method could not predict.

5. Wrong idea corrected: the first-principles number is a limit, not a target

Common wrong idea

Wrong: "First principles show that a battery costs $80 per kWh, so a company that charges $115 is inefficient or greedy."

Correct: $80 per kWh is the cost of the materials alone. The number says that a price below $80 is impossible. It does not say that a price of $80 is possible. Factories, energy, labour, transport, failed cells, research, and profit all sit between the limit and the price.

Why the wrong idea fails: the method removes everything that is not a verified fact. So the result omits every cost that depends on a process. The gap between the limit and the price is the space in which engineering works. A gap of 1.4 (section 4) is small. A gap of 7.5 was large, and that was the point of the example.

A second wrong idea is the opposite: "First-principles reasoning means that I ignore what experts know." That is also false. The facts in step 2 come from experts: material prices, disk speeds, laws of physics. The method rejects only beliefs without facts under them. And Musk himself said that you reason by analogy for most of the day, and that you must.

6. Limits: the method is only as good as the facts that you choose

Limits
  • The limit depends on the chosen facts. Musk chose material prices in 2012. Material prices change: BNEF names low metal prices as one reason for the fall in 2024. A limit from 2012 is not a limit for 2030.
  • A missed fact gives a wrong limit. In section 3, a reader who forgets the disk gets the wrong limit when the link is 10 gigabits per second. The method needs a complete list of the parts, and the list is a judgment.
  • The method is slow. Each fact must be verified. For a small decision, the cost of the method is higher than the cost of a wrong analogy.
  • The numbers of the battery example are not fully verified. The $600 and $80 come from Musk in an interview, as reported by CNBC. Neither number has a published breakdown. Their confidence is Medium. The BNEF numbers are from press releases; the full reports are not public.
  • The 2010 price and the later prices use different dollars. BNEF gives the 2010 value in real terms and the 2020 and 2024 values as reported. The chart mixes them. The ratio 1,100 to 137 is therefore approximate.

7. Words that you will hear

These terms appear in the same conversations as first-principles reasoning. They are not needed for the main idea.

Fermi estimate
A fast estimate of a quantity from a few known facts and rough numbers, with a goal of the right order of magnitude. The example in section 3 is a Fermi estimate. A Fermi estimate is first-principles reasoning with low precision.
Axiom
In mathematics, a statement that is accepted without proof, and from which the other statements are proved. An axiom is a first principle in the strict sense. In engineering, the first principles are measurements and laws, not axioms.
Ab initio
Latin for "from the beginning". In chemistry and physics, an ab initio calculation uses only the laws of physics and no measured parameters. It is first-principles reasoning in its most strict form.
Lower bound
In mathematics and computer science, a value that a quantity cannot go below. The $80 per kWh in section 1 and the 8,000 seconds in section 3 are lower bounds. "Limit" on this page means a lower bound.
Chesterton's fence
A rule of caution: do not remove a fence before you know why it was built. It warns against step 1 done badly. A belief can look like an analogy and still have a good reason that nobody wrote down.

8. Check your understanding

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. A colleague says: "A 50 gigabyte backup over our 100 megabit per second line takes about 4 hours." What is the limit from first principles? Use 1 gigabyte = 109 bytes.
    Show the answer

    4,000 seconds, which is 66.7 minutes, about 1 hour 7 minutes.

    50 × 109 bytes × 8 = 4 × 1011 bits. 4 × 1011 ÷ 108 bits per second = 4,000 seconds. The claim of 4 hours is 3.6 times the limit. A typical wrong answer is 500 seconds: that answer forgets the factor 8 between bytes and bits. See section 3.

  2. Which of these two statements uses analogy, and which uses first principles? (A) "Our last service used 4 servers, so this one needs 4 servers." (B) "Each request needs 20 milliseconds of one processor core, and we expect 1,000 requests per second, so we need 20 cores."
    Show the answer

    (A) is analogy. (B) is first-principles reasoning.

    (A) copies a known case without a check that the new case is similar. (B) starts from two facts, a measured cost per request and an expected rate, and multiplies: 1,000 × 0.020 seconds = 20 core-seconds per second = 20 cores. (B) is a lower bound: the real need is higher, because a core cannot be 100% busy. See section 2.

  3. In 2024 the average pack price was $115 per kWh, and the material cost in 2012 was $80 per kWh. Does the method say that the price will reach $80?
    Show the answer

    No.

    The method gives a limit from below. It says that a price below the material cost is impossible. It does not say that the material cost is reachable, because every process adds cost above the materials. Also, $80 was the material cost in 2012, and material prices change. See section 5.

9. Recap, glossary, and sources

Recap
  1. First-principles reasoning has four steps: write the claim and its belief, decompose into verified facts, calculate the limit, compare the limit with the current state.
  2. The result is a lower bound, not a prediction and not a target. The gap between the bound and the current state measures the room for improvement.
  3. Battery example: $600 per kWh in 2012, material cost $80, ratio 7.5. Measured price in 2024: $115, ratio 1.4.
  4. Use analogy by default. Use first principles when a wrong belief is expensive, or when someone says "that is impossible".

Glossary

Analogy
A comparison with a known case: the new case is treated as similar to the known case. A comparison, not a proof.
First principle
A fact that you accept without a further proof in the current problem: a law of physics, a measurement, a public price, or a definition.
Kilowatt-hour (kWh)
A unit of energy: the energy that a 1,000 watt device uses in one hour.
Reasoning by analogy
The method of copying a known solution with small changes.
