What makes a website engaging, so that a reader clicks and explores? The evidence, applied to aiolum.com

Written 2026-10-29 · Reading time: about 25 minutes · You need to know: what a page view and a visit are

The short answer

A reader clicks a link for two reasons. The link lets them predict what is behind it. And the click opens a question that they believe they can close. The two best supported mechanisms are information scent and the information gap. Scent means that the reader estimates the value of a link from its label and its context. A gap is a question that feels important, salient and surprising.

Readers explore in two different ways. Hunters follow one direct path to one answer. Busybodies jump between loosely connected novelties. A site that wants exploration must serve both: ranked answers and search for the hunter, unexpected neighbours on every page for the busybody.

Visual style and introductory stories have no measured effect on exploration. The measured effects come from link labels, previews, and the quality of list pages. For aiolum.com, the baseline is 1.34 pages per visit (336 page views in 251 visits, 22 to 29 October 2026). Most readers read one page and leave.

Assumptions on this page
  • "Engaging" means: the reader opens more than one page, and the pages that they open differ from each other. The page measures this with pages per visit and distinct pages per visit.
  • The findings come from studies of news sites, Wikipedia and online shops. aiolum.com is a catalogue of 252 verified stories, so the findings transfer. But no study was made on aiolum.com itself.
  • Confidence labels follow the rules in the sources section. A study on a large site counts as High. A theory with one large observational test counts as Medium.

1. The baseline: aiolum.com has 1.34 pages per visit

A visit is one session of one browser on the site. A page view is one page that the browser loaded in that session. The ratio of page views to visits is pages per visit. A value of 1.00 means that every reader read exactly one page and left. The Cloudflare Web Analytics numbers for aiolum.com, read on 29 October 2026, give the following baseline.

How to read this table: pages per visit = page views ÷ visits = 336 ÷ 251 = 1.339, rounded to 1.34. Bots are excluded. Counting began on 21 October 2026, so no earlier week exists for comparison.

The number says that the site wins the first page view but rarely the second. Every finding below is about the second click: why a reader takes it, and what stops them.

2. A reader clicks when the label predicts the page: information scent

The Nielsen Norman Group (from here: NN/g) describes the behaviour of a reader with the model of information foraging. The model says that a reader behaves like an animal that searches for food. Before each move, the reader estimates the expected value of the move and its cost in time. The estimate comes from information scent: the clues that a link gives about the page behind it. There are three clues. The first is the label of the link. The second is the text and the images around the link. The third is the trust from earlier clicks on the same site.

Worked example: the same story, two labels
  1. A story tile on aiolum.com says "Base44" with a logo and the button "Read the story". The label gives no clue about the outcome, the time, or the proof. The reader cannot estimate the value of the click, so the expected value is low.
  2. The same tile says "Base44: sold to Wix for US$80M, 5 months after launch. Grade A: the price is in the buyer's own filing." The label gives the outcome, the time and the proof. The reader can now estimate the value of the click. They also learn one fact without a click, which NN/g calls a label that adds information.
  3. The reader clicks, and the page opens with the same fact in the first sentence. The page confirms the promise quickly. Trust grows, and the next link on the same site gets a higher estimate.

Result: the second label is longer, but it carries scent. The first label asks the reader to pay the cost of a click before they know the value.

NN/g adds a warning about clickbait, that is, a label that promises more than the page delivers. Such a label wins one click. NN/g writes that the reader will then be less likely to click a later link from the same source, because they were deceived before. For a site that sells trust in verified numbers, this warning matters more than for a news site.

Weak scent

"Read the story". "Learn more". A logo without text. A tile with a large image and three words.

The reader must click to find the value.

Strong scent

"Sold for US$80M after 5 months, grade A". "Tried the same idea and failed: 5 companies". "Runs on Supabase: 46 stories".

The reader knows the value before the click.

3. Curiosity is a gap that feels important, salient, surprising and closeable

Loewenstein proposed in 1994 the information-gap theory of curiosity. The theory says that curiosity is the discomfort that a person feels when they know a part of something and notice the missing part. The missing part is the gap.

Golman and Loewenstein later made the theory precise. The strength of the curiosity depends on three properties of the question. The question is important when the answer matters to the person. The question is salient when it occupies the attention of the person right now. The question has surprise when the answer promises to differ from what the person expects. The gap must also look closeable. A gap that is too small produces no curiosity, and a gap that looks impossible to close makes the reader stop.

Three studies test the theory with real behaviour.

  • Golman, Loewenstein, Molnar and Saccardo ran five experiments with 2,361 participants in total. People paid more to see information when the question felt more important, even when the information could not change any decision. They also paid more when the question was more salient, and when they expected good news.
  • Qiu and Golman analysed more than 100,000 news articles on WeChat, a Chinese messaging platform, and predicted clicks from the headline. Clicks rose in four cases. The headline raised a salient question. The content appeared more important. The topic was more surprising. Or the headline was more negative. The same study found a small negative correlation between the number of clicks and the ratio of likes to clicks. Curiosity drives the click, but it does not by itself make the reader like the page.
  • Molnar and Golman ran two experiments with 2,406 participants. A delay before the answer reduced the willingness to obtain information, even when the delay did not change the value of the information. Curiosity is impatient.
Worked example: the gap that aiolum.com already owns
  1. In July 2026 the founder of MagicSlides wrote that the product makes around US$19,000 a month. The public revenue profile shows US$9,623 for July 2026, and aiolum.com verified that number. The two numbers sit next to each other on the tile, each with its grade. The claim has grade C, and the verified number has grade A. The reader knows a part (the claim) and sees a missing part (what was true). The gap is salient because both numbers are on the screen.
  2. The tile states that the answer is on the page: "What the records show". The gap looks closeable. The reader expects an answer, not a sales page.
  3. The story page gives the answer in the first paragraph, and then the method. The answer is immediate. A delay, for example a long introduction before the number, reduces the willingness to continue (Molnar and Golman).

Result: the gap is honest, because the page closes it with a verified number. This is the difference between a curiosity gap and clickbait: the page delivers the answer that the label promised.

4. Readers explore as hunters or as busybodies, and a site must serve both

Zhou and colleagues at the University of Pennsylvania studied the reading paths of more than 480,000 Wikipedia readers in 14 languages and 50 countries. They described each reader by the knowledge network of their session: the articles that the reader opened, connected by the links between those articles. Two styles appeared in every language.

  • A hunter follows a direct path from one article to the next in search of one specific answer. The knowledge network of a hunter is tight: the articles are close to each other.
  • A busybody collects loose pieces of novelty. The knowledge network of a busybody is wide: the articles are far from each other, and the reader jumps between them.

The study also found a third style, the dancer, who jumps between areas of knowledge that are normally separate. This style was harder to measure and the evidence for it is weaker. The authors suggest a design change. Instead of a list of popular or similar pages, a site could show readers their own knowledge network. The readers would then see the path that they already walked. This suggestion was not tested.

For aiolum.com, the two styles want two different kinds of next click. A diagram shows the structure that the story page needs.

5. Measured features at Wikimedia: previews widen reading, links add 1.4% traffic

The Wikimedia Foundation tests features with A/B tests. In such a test, half of the readers get the feature and half do not, and the two groups are compared. The result comes with a 95% confidence interval: the range in which the true effect lies with 95% probability, given the data. Two results are documented in public.

The Page Previews result is the most useful one for aiolum.com. A preview lets a reader examine a neighbour without the cost of a page load. The reader then opens fewer pages, but a wider set of pages. So a site that wants exploration should measure distinct pages per visit, not only page views. A rise in exploration can appear as a fall in page views.

6. On a list page, the tools decide whether the reader finds anything

Baymard Institute tested 19 large online shops with users between 21 and 56 years old. The study is about product lists: pages that show many items with tools to filter and sort them. Sites with a mediocre list saw abandonment of 67% to 90% of the users who searched for a given type of product. Sites with a slightly better set of tools saw abandonment of 17% to 33% for the same task. Baymard writes that this is as much as a 4-fold difference in the number of users who reach a product.

aiolum.com has one product list: the Stories tab with 252 stories. The task of a hunter on that page is a question. Two examples: "Which solo founder reached US$100,000 fastest?" and "Which stories have grade A?" The Baymard result says that the sort and filter tools for these questions decide the outcome. The hunter finds an answer, or the hunter leaves. On the current site, the filters exist, but they sit below the first screen.

A second old but often replicated finding supports short labels on list pages. In 1997 NN/g observed that 79% of their test users scanned every new page, and only 16% read word by word. A list page is scanned, so each row must give its fact in the first words.

7. What did not work: a story introduction and a bigger search box

Common wrong idea

Wrong: "A good introduction, or a story that leads into the data, makes readers explore the data."

Correct: Boy, Detienne and Fekete ran three field experiments on a news site and on a visualization gallery. They added introductory stories to exploratory visualizations. Against their own hypothesis, the stories did not increase exploration.

Why the wrong idea fails: the introduction tells the reader what to see, so the reader has no gap left to close. Exploration needs a question that the reader owns, and an object that answers on the first click.

Two more negative results come from Wikimedia. A more prominent search bar in the iOS app increased the number of searches but did not increase article reading. And the Page Previews feature reduced page views while it widened reading. Both results say the same thing: a feature changes where the reader goes, and the metric must match the goal.

No controlled study of "related content" widgets was found. The only numbers come from vendors of such widgets. One vendor reports a rise from 1.4 to 1.9 pages per session on two sites, without a control group. This page treats that claim as unproven.

9. Limits

Limits
  • No study was made on aiolum.com. All findings come from news sites, Wikipedia and online shops. The transfer to a catalogue of 252 stories is a judgment, not a measurement.
  • The baseline is one week. 251 visits is a small number. The pages-per-visit value will move by several hundredths from week to week without any change to the site.
  • The curiosity gap has a cost. Qiu and Golman found that articles with more clicks had a slightly lower ratio of likes to clicks. A gap that the page does not close honestly will reduce trust, and NN/g says that lost trust lowers later clicks.
  • The design suggestion of Zhou and colleagues was not tested. The two reader styles are measured. The idea to show readers their own path is a proposal.
  • The "related content" numbers from vendors have no control group. They are listed as unproven.
  • The 1997 scanning study is old. Later eye-tracking studies by NN/g confirm the pattern, but the exact percentages come from one study with a small number of users.

10. Check your understanding

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. A story tile on aiolum.com says "Chatbase" with a logo and "Read the story". Which finding says that this tile loses clicks, and how would you rewrite it?
    Show the answer

    Information scent. The label gives no clue about the value behind the click. Rewrite it with the fact and the grade, for example "Chatbase: US$10M annual recurring revenue, March 2026, grade A". A typical wrong answer is "make the tile bigger": size adds no scent.

  2. After the redesign, page views per visit fall from 1.34 to 1.25, but visits with 2 or more distinct pages rise. Did exploration fall or rise?
    Show the answer

    Exploration rose. The Wikimedia Page Previews result shows the same pattern: fewer page views, more distinct pages. If previews let readers examine neighbours without a page load, page views fall while the set of pages that a reader touches grows. The typical wrong answer reads the fall in page views as a loss.

  3. Which two numbers on a tile make an honest curiosity gap, and what must the page then do?
    Show the answer

    The claim and the verified number, each with its grade. The page must close the gap in its first paragraph with the verified number and its source. If the page delays the answer, Molnar and Golman say that willingness falls, and if the page does not deliver, NN/g says that trust falls.

11. Recap, glossary, and sources

Recap
  1. A reader clicks a link when its label and context let them predict the value of the page. A label that carries a fact wins more clicks than a generic label.
  2. Curiosity is a gap that feels important, salient and surprising, and that looks closeable. aiolum.com owns such a gap in every story. The gap is the claim against the verified number.
  3. Hunters want a ranked answer and search. Busybodies want unexpected neighbours. Every story page needs both kinds of exit.
  4. Previews widen reading and can lower page views, so measure distinct pages per visit. The baseline is 1.34 pages per visit.
  5. Visual style and introductory stories have no measured effect on exploration.

Glossary

95% confidence interval
The range in which the true effect lies with 95% probability, given the data of the test.
A/B test
An experiment in which half of the readers get a feature and half do not, and the two groups are compared.
Busybody
A reader who collects loose pieces of novelty and jumps between articles that are far from each other.
Clickbait
A link label that promises more than the page delivers.
Dancer
A reader who jumps between areas of knowledge that are normally separate. The evidence for this style is weaker.
Hunter
A reader who follows a direct path from one article to the next in search of one specific answer.
Information foraging
A model of reading in which the reader estimates the value and the cost of each move before they make it.
Information-gap theory
The theory that curiosity is the discomfort of a known part next to a missing part.
Information scent
The clues that a link gives about the page behind it: its label, its context, and the trust from earlier clicks.
Knowledge network
The set of articles that a reader opened in one session, connected by the links between them.
Page view
One page that a browser loaded in one visit.
Pages per visit
Page views divided by visits.
Product list
A page that shows many items with tools to filter and sort them.
Visit
One session of one browser on the site.
