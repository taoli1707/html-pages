Why does a steady sound help you sleep through noise, and how can you measure whether it works?

Written 2026-10-06 · Reading time: about 26 minutes · You need to know: what a decibel is (the unit of loudness; 10 dB more sounds about twice as loud)

The short answer

A steady sound does not cancel noise. It raises the background, so that a sudden noise is a smaller step above the background. In a sleep laboratory, the size of that step decided whether a sleeper woke: about 17.5 dB, the same with and without white noise. With recorded intensive-care noise, sleepers had 48.4 arousals per hour; with white noise added, 15.7, close to the quiet night's 13.3.

The second effect, masking, is that a sound hides another sound of a similar pitch. Low pitches hide high pitches better than the reverse, so the steady sound must be shaped to the noise you want to hide.

To measure whether it works, measure two things. First, the step at your pillow, with a sound-level app; the best phone apps are within ±2 dB. Second, your sleep over three weeks with a diary and a wrist tracker: one week without the sound, one with, one without. The research evidence for white noise as a sleep aid is rated very low in quality. So your own three weeks are the test that counts.

Assumptions on this page
  • The sleeper is an adult. The infant limit of 50 dBA appears only in the safety section.
  • The example night (a snore of 55 dBA over a background of 32 dBA) uses example values. The laboratory numbers are from the published studies.
  • "White noise" on this page means any steady broadband sound: white, pink or brown noise, a fan, rain. The studies used various kinds.

Part A

Why a steady sound helps: the step, and masking

The short answer for part A

Sleepers wake when a sound rises about 17.5 dB above the background. A steady sound raises the background, so the same noise is a smaller step. And a sound hides noise of a similar pitch, more so from below than from above.

1. One laboratory night: 48.4 arousals per hour fell to 15.7

In 2005 a group at Brown Medical School recorded five healthy adults under three conditions. They used polysomnography (the full sleep recording of brain waves, eye movement and muscle tone). An arousal is a brief awakening of the brain, seen in the brain waves, which the sleeper usually does not remember. The arousal index is the number of arousals per hour of sleep.

Worked example: the Brown study (Stanchina and colleagues, 2005)
  1. Quiet night: 13.3 arousals per hour. Everyone has some arousals; this is the baseline for these sleepers.
  2. Recorded intensive-care-unit noise played in the room: 48.4 arousals per hour, 3.6 times the baseline. Intensive-care noise is a series of sudden sounds: alarms, voices, doors. Each sudden sound can cause an arousal.
  3. The same recorded noise plus steady white noise: 15.7 arousals per hour. The extra arousals fell by 68%. The white noise did not remove the alarms. It raised the background, so each alarm was a smaller step above it.
  4. The step from the background to the peak that caused an arousal was 17.7 dB without white noise and 17.5 dB with it. The peak alone did not predict arousals. The threshold for the brain is the change, not the absolute loudness. White noise moves the starting point of that change.

Result: one steady sound removed 68% of the extra arousals, because it changed the step and not the peak.

2. The rule: the step from background to peak decides, not the peak

Levels on this page are in dBA (A-weighted decibels: loudness weighted by pitch the way the ear hears it). Every limit quoted here uses this scale.

The background level is the steady loudness of the room between events. The peak level is the loudness of a sudden event, for example one snore. The rule from the laboratory: an arousal happens when the peak is about 17.5 dB above the background. A steady sound raises the background. Therefore the same peak is a smaller step, and fewer peaks cross the threshold.

Levels in dB do not add like ordinary numbers, because the scale is logarithmic. Two sounds of 40 dB together give 43 dB, not 80 dB. The table gives the sums that this page uses.

The formula is: together = 10 × log10(10^(a/10) + 10^(b/10)). A sound 13 dB quieter than another adds only 0.2 dB. That is why a steady sound at 45 dBA makes a 32 dBA room a 45 dBA room.

3. Masking is per pitch: low hides high, not the reverse

The step rule is about loudness over time. Auditory masking is about pitch: one sound makes another sound of a similar pitch inaudible, or quieter than it is. In 1940 Fletcher showed that only the part of the masking sound within a narrow range of pitch around the target sound counts. That range is the critical band (the width of the ear's own filter around one pitch). Energy outside the band does not help.

The effect is not symmetric. A low-pitched sound masks higher pitches over a wide range; a high-pitched sound masks only pitches near its own. This is called the upward spread of masking. The consequence for sleep: a snore, which is mostly low-pitched, is hidden only by a sound with enough energy at low pitches. A hiss-like sound at the same total level leaves the snore audible.

White noise

Equal energy per hertz. Each octave band is twice as wide as the one below it, so white noise is 3 dB louder in each higher octave band. From the 63 Hz band to the 8 kHz band it rises by 21 dB. It sounds like a hiss, and most of its energy is high-pitched.

Pink noise

Equal energy per octave band. Each band carries the same level, so the sound is balanced from low to high. It sounds like steady rain or a waterfall. Brown noise goes further: 3 dB less in each higher band, a rumble.

The principle: at the same total level, the sound that hides a snore is the one with its energy at the snore's pitch. That is why the app on this site measures the surroundings in 8 octave bands and shapes its sound band by band. The page How does Adapt measure your surroundings and shape the sound? explains that mechanism.

4. What the studies found, and how weak the evidence is

Three studies with sleep measurements, not only questionnaires:

  • Brown, 2005: five healthy adults, polysomnography, recorded intensive-care noise. Arousals per hour 13.3, 48.4 and 15.7, as above.
  • Harvard, 2017 (Messineo and colleagues): 18 healthy adults, two nights, bedtime moved 90 minutes earlier to model insomnia. Room noise 40.1 dB, or broadband sound at 46.0 dB from two speakers. Time to fall asleep (to stage 2) was 19 minutes with room noise and 13 minutes with the sound. The paper reports a median reduction of 38%.
  • New York, 2021 (Ebben and colleagues): 10 adults with sleep complaints in noisy homes. One week without a white-noise machine, one week with, one week without. Time awake in the night (measured by a wrist tracker) fell, and self-reported time to fall asleep fell. Both were statistically significant with 10 people.

Against that, the 2021 systematic review of 38 studies (Riedy and colleagues) rated the quality of evidence that continuous noise improves sleep as very low. The studies differed in the sound, the measurement and the control condition, and the results ranged from improved to disrupted sleep. The review also noted a possible negative effect on sleep and on hearing. So: the mechanism is clear and measured, the population benefit is not established, and whether it helps you is a question for part B.

5. Wrong idea corrected: white noise does not "block" noise

Common wrong idea

Wrong: "White noise blocks or cancels the noise, so the snore gets quieter."

Correct: the snore arrives at your ear at the same level. Two things change. The step from background to peak is smaller. And the part of the snore within the masked pitch range is harder to hear.

Why the wrong idea fails: cancellation needs a second sound with the opposite waveform at the ear, which is what noise-cancelling headphones do. A speaker in the room cannot do that. In the Brown study the peaks were the same on both nights. Only the background changed, and the arousals fell from 48.4 to 15.7 per hour.

6. Check your understanding, part A

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. The background in a room is 40 dBA. A door closes at 56 dBA. You add a steady sound at 50 dBA. Is the step now above or below the 17.5 dB threshold?
    Show the answer

    Below. The step is about 5.6 dB.

    Background with the sound: 40 dBA + 50 dBA together = 50.4 dBA (table in section 2). Step: 56 − 50.4 = 5.6 dB. Without the sound the step was 16 dB, already close to the threshold. A typical wrong answer adds 40 + 50 = 90 dB; levels do not add that way.

  2. Two sounds have the same total level. One is a hiss, one is a rumble. Which hides a snore better, and why?
    Show the answer

    The rumble.

    A snore is low-pitched. Masking works within the critical band around the snore's pitch, and low pitches mask upward, not downward. The hiss has its energy at high pitches, outside the snore's band. See section 3. A typical wrong answer is "the same, because the level is the same": total level says nothing about where the energy is.

Part B

How to measure whether it works

The short answer for part B

Measure the step at your pillow with a phone app: background, peak, and both again with the sound on. Then measure your sleep for three weeks, one change in the middle week, with a diary and a wrist tracker. And check the level at the pillow for safety.

7. Measure the step at the pillow: 23 dB becomes 10 dB

A sound-level app turns the phone into a sound-level meter. In a NIOSH test of 130 iOS apps, the three best were within ±2 dB of a reference meter, from 65 to 95 dB. With an external calibrated microphone the error fell to ±1 dB. Bedroom levels are lower than 65 dB, so treat the readings as approximate, and compare readings from the same phone in the same place. Set the app to dBA.

Worked example: one evening of measurements
  1. Put the phone on the pillow, screen up, for 5 minutes with the room as it is at night. Read the lowest steady value: background 32 dBA. The pillow is where the ear will be. The lowest steady value is the background, not the average, because the average includes the peaks.
  2. Wait for the noise you want to hide, or play a recording of it from where it comes from. Read the highest value: peak 55 dBA. The step is 55 − 32 = 23 dB. 23 dB is above the 17.5 dB threshold, so this noise can cause arousals.
  3. Turn on the steady sound at the level you would sleep with. Read the new background: 45 dBA. The app reads the sum of the room and the sound: 32 and 45 together are 45.2 dBA.
  4. Peak again with the sound on: still about 55 dBA, because the snore has not changed. New step: 55 − 45 = 10 dB. 10 dB is under 17.5 dB. The sound is loud enough to take this noise under the threshold.
  5. Calculate the lowest sound level that would do it: the background must reach 55 − 17.5 = 37.5 dBA. With a 32 dBA room, a sound of 36.1 dBA is enough. 10 × log10(10^3.75 − 10^3.2) = 36.1. Anything above this is louder than it needs to be for this noise.

Result: for this noise, the sound needs to be at least 36 dBA at the pillow; 45 dBA gives a 7.5 dB reserve.

This is the step rule only. For masking by pitch, the phone app shows one number, not a spectrum. The app on this site does the per-pitch version automatically. It measures the surroundings in 8 bands, and its Coverage sheet reports how far its sound sits above each band. A good manual check is simpler: lie down, play the sound, and ask whether you can still hear the noise. If you can hear it clearly, the sound is wrong in pitch or in level.

8. Measure your sleep: three weeks, one change

The step measurement predicts fewer arousals. Whether you sleep better is a separate question, and the only honest way to answer it is to measure your own sleep. The New York study used an ABA design. ABA is a test in three periods: A, a week without the change; B, a week with it; A again, a week without it. The return to the first condition shows whether an improvement came from the sound or from something else that week.

The three-week test, as the New York study ran it

Keep bedtime, alcohol and caffeine the same across the three weeks, or write them down. Compare the averages of week 2 with weeks 1 and 3.

The two numbers that moved in the New York study are the two to watch. Wake after sleep onset (from here: WASO; the minutes awake between falling asleep and the final waking) fell, as measured by the wrist tracker. Sleep latency (the minutes from lights out to falling asleep) fell, as reported in the diary. A wrist tracker measures movement, so its WASO is approximate; it is the same approximation in all three weeks, which is what matters.

What counts as "it works": week 2 is better than both week 1 and week 3, on WASO or on sleep latency. The difference must be larger than the night-to-night variation inside week 1. With 7 nights per week, a change of a few minutes is noise. A change of 10 minutes or more in WASO, seen in both comparisons, is a result.

9. Measure the level for safety: stay well under 50 dBA at the pillow

The same app gives the safety check. Three reference values, all in dBA:

A steady sound for sleep sits between 35 and 50 dBA at the pillow in the examples on this page. The Harvard study used 46 dB. A machine at full volume next to a cot can exceed 85 dBA, which is a hearing risk over a night.

10. Check your understanding, part B

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. Your background is 35 dBA and the loudest truck is 60 dBA. What is the lowest steady sound level at the pillow that brings the step under 17.5 dB?
    Show the answer

    About 42 dBA.

    The background must reach 60 − 17.5 = 42.5 dBA. The sound alone must be 10 × log10(10^4.25 − 10^3.5) = 41.7 dBA, because the room's own 35 dBA adds a little. A typical wrong answer is 42.5 − 35 = 7.5 dBA; levels do not subtract that way. See section 7.

  2. In your three-week test, WASO is 40 minutes in week 1, 25 in week 2 and 38 in week 3. Does the sound work?
    Show the answer

    Yes, by this test.

    Week 2 is 15 minutes better than week 1 and 13 minutes better than week 3. The return to about 40 in week 3 shows the improvement followed the sound and not the calendar. A typical wrong conclusion compares only weeks 1 and 2; without week 3 you cannot tell a real effect from a good week. See section 8.

11. Limits

Limits
  • The 17.5 dB threshold comes from 5 healthy adults. Light sleepers and children may wake at smaller steps; the number is a guide, not a law.
  • The evidence for better sleep is very low quality. The 2021 review of 38 studies found results from improved to disrupted sleep. The three studies on this page are small: 5, 18 and 10 people.
  • Phone sound-level apps were tested from 65 to 95 dB. Bedroom levels are lower, where the phone's microphone is less accurate. Use the readings to compare, not as absolute values.
  • The step rule ignores pitch. A 10 dB step at the snore's pitch is one thing. A 10 dB step at a pitch the sound does not cover is another, and the ear hears the difference. The lying-down listening check covers this.
  • Hearing. A sound played all night near 50 dBA is far below the occupational limit. But the review names hearing as an open concern, and no long-term study was found.
