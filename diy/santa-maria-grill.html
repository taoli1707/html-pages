How do you build a Santa Maria grill?

Written 2026-10-13 · Reading time: about 33 minutes, main path about 25 minutes · You need to know: basic metalwork words (weld, angle iron, square tube)

The short answer

A Santa Maria grill is an open wood fire with a cooking grate that you raise and lower with a hand wheel. The grate height above the coals is the only heat control. You build one from four parts: a steel firebox with a firebrick floor, a frame of square tube, a heavy grate, and a lift. The lift is a shaft with two cable drums, two cables, and a hand wheel.

The design on this page: a grate of 36 × 24 inches that moves from 4 to 24 inches above the coals. The frame is 70 inches tall. The lift carries about 59 lb with food. Each 1/8-inch stainless cable carries about 30 lb, 1.7% of its 1,760 lb breaking strength. A 14-inch hand wheel needs about 8 lb of hand force.

The one physical fact to keep. Above a 36 × 24 inch bed of coals, the radiant heat at 12 inches is about 2.0 times the heat at 24 inches. It is not 4 times. The inverse-square rule does not apply to a large coal bed.

Assumptions on this page
  • You can weld mild steel, or you can give drawings to a welder. The masonry version in section 8 needs less welding.
  • The grill stands outdoors, on a surface that does not burn, at least 10 feet (3 m) from a wooden wall or deck (section 4).
  • The coal bed in the heat calculation is at 700 °C (1,292 °F), an example value (section 3).
  • Dimensions are in inches, as in every source. 1 inch = 2.54 cm.

1. What a Santa Maria grill is: an open fire, a grate, and a lift

A Santa Maria grill is an open grill with no lid. The food cooks on a cooking grate (a flat grid of steel bars, from here: grate). The grate hangs above a coal bed (a layer of glowing wood coals, 2 to 4 inches deep). The fire burns in a firebox (an open steel box or brick pit that holds the wood, the coals, and the ash). The lift moves the grate: a shaft, two cable drums, two cables, and a hand wheel. A ratchet (a toothed wheel) with a pawl (a small lever that blocks the teeth in one direction) holds the grate at any height.

The grill comes from the Santa Maria Valley in California, where ranchers cooked beef over pits of coals in the 19th century (source 1). The cook burns the wood down to coals first, then cooks over the coals, not over flames (source 2).

The lift is the only part with a load calculation.

Worked example: the load on the lift, and the force on the wheel
  1. Steel weighs 7,850 kg/m³, which is 490 lb per cubic foot, or 0.2836 lb per cubic inch. Every weight below is volume × 0.2836. Real steel varies by about 1% (source 9).
  2. One grate bar: 3/8 in diameter, 24 in long. Volume = π × 0.1875² × 24 = 2.65 in³. Weight = 0.75 lb.
  3. Number of bars across 36 in with 1/2 in gaps: the pitch is 0.375 + 0.5 = 0.875 in. Bars = 41. Weight of all bars = 41 × 0.75 = 30.8 lb. (36 − 0.375) ÷ 0.875 = 40.7, so 40 gaps and 41 bars.
  4. Grate frame: 1 × 1 × 1/8 in angle has a cross-section of 0.234 in², so it weighs 0.80 lb per foot. 10 ft of frame: 8.0 lb.
  5. Two lift arms (1.5 × 1.5 × 3/16 in angle, 8 in each): 2.4 lb. Two guide plates (1/4 in plate, 6 × 4 in, with a notch): 2.8 lb. Hanging steel: 30.8 + 8.0 + 2.4 + 2.8 = 44.0 lb.
  6. Add 15 lb of food. Load on the lift: 59 lb. Four tri-tips of 2.5 lb are 10 lb. 15 lb leaves a margin.
  7. Each cable carries half: 29.5 lb. The cable is 1/8 in stainless steel aircraft cable of 7 strands with 19 wires each (7×19). Its minimum breaking strength (the smallest force at which a new cable breaks in a test) is 1,760 lb (source 10). The load is 1.7% of it. Suppliers set the working load limit at one fifth of the breaking strength, here 352 lb. The margin is deliberate: heat and corrosion weaken the cable over years.
  8. Torque on the shaft: 59 lb × drum radius 1 in = 59 lb·in. Force at the rim of a 14 in wheel (radius 7 in): 59 ÷ 7 = 8.4 lb. The ratio of the radii, 7 to 1, divides the force by 7.
  9. Turns of the wheel for the full travel of 20 in: 20 ÷ (2π × 1 in) = 3.2 turns. One turn winds one circumference of the drum.

Result: about 8 lb of hand force, and 3.2 turns for the full travel.

The drawing shows steps 8 and 9 as a lever.

3. The rule: the grate height sets the radiant heat, and a large coal bed falls off slowly

Heat reaches the food as hot air and as thermal radiation (infrared light that carries heat through the air without contact). Over an open fire, the hot air spreads sideways. The radiation is the part that the height controls in a predictable way, so this section calculates the radiation only.

Two things set the radiant heat at the grate. The first is the power that the coal bed sends out. The Stefan-Boltzmann law gives this power: it grows with the fourth power of the absolute temperature. The second is the view factor: the fraction of that radiation that arrives at a given point. The view factor depends only on geometry: the size of the coal bed and the height of the grate.

For the 36 × 24 in coal bed at 700 °C, the result is 14.4 kW/m² at 24 in and 29.1 kW/m² at 12 in. The ratio is 2.03, so half the height gives about 2 times the heat. The reason is the view factor: at 12 in the grate already sees the coal bed under a wide angle. Section 7 shows each step of the calculation.

4. The build, in 9 steps and 5 drawings

  1. Draw the grill at full size on the floor or on cardboard. Mark the firebox (40.5 × 27 in inside), the grate (36 × 24 in), and the two posts, 48 in apart. The drawing shows every clearance before you cut steel.
  2. Cut and weld the firebox from 11-gauge mild steel sheet: a floor of 40.5 × 27 in and four walls 10 in high. Weld the outside corners with a continuous bead. Drill three holes of 1/2 in in each long wall, 1 in above the firebrick floor. A continuous bead keeps ash and water inside. The holes feed air to the coal bed.
  1. Weld the frame from 2 × 2 × 1/8 in square tube. The base is a rectangle of rails, 48 × 28 in, on four legs of 24 in. A post of 70 in stands at the middle of each end. Add two cross members under the firebox, and two braces from the legs to each post. The base carries 83 lb of firebox and 260 lb of brick. The posts carry the lift, and the braces stop them from leaning.
  1. Build the grate: weld a rectangle of 1 × 1 × 1/8 in angle, 36 × 24 in outside. Weld 41 bars of 3/8 in round bar across the short direction with 1/2 in gaps. Weld a lift arm of 1.5 × 1.5 × 3/16 in angle, 8 in tall, on the middle of each end. Bars across 24 in sag less than bars across 36 in.
  2. Add guides. Weld a plate of 1/4 in steel, 6 × 4 in, to the top of each lift arm. Cut a notch of 2.1 in in the plate, so that the plate fits around the post. Without guides, the grate swings when you push a steak.
  1. Build the lift. Drill a hole through each post at 69 in, and bolt a flange bearing on the outer face. Slide the 3/4 in shaft through, with a drum of 2 in diameter above each lift arm. Fix the 14 in hand wheel and the ratchet on one end, and the pawl on the post. Run a cable from each drum to a shackle on the lift arm, and close each cable end with two clamps. The pawl holds the grate at every height. Both cables must wind in the same direction, or one end rises while the other end falls.
  1. Lay the firebrick. Place 27 full bricks (9 × 4.5 × 2.5 in) flat on the floor, 9 across and 3 deep, without mortar. Lay 14 half bricks (9 × 4.5 × 1.25 in) on edge along the walls: 3 on each short wall, 4 on each long wall. The steel walls hold the bricks, so no mortar is needed. The brick stores heat and protects the steel floor.
  2. Finish the outside only. Grind the welds. Paint the frame and the outside of the firebox with a paint rated for at least 1,200 °F (650 °C). Leave the grate and the inside of the firebox bare. Paint on the inside burns off in the first fire. Oil on the grate, heated, forms the dark layer that stops rust.
  3. Make a first fire without food. Burn 10 lb of wood to coals, then move the grate through the full travel 10 times. Check that the cables stay in the grooves, that the grate stays level, and that the pawl holds at every tooth. A fault in the lift is dangerous once the grate carries hot food.

Two safety rules that the build depends on

Do not weld or heat galvanized steel. The zinc coating vaporizes to zinc oxide fume, which causes metal fume fever (source 13). Use bare mild steel for every part that gets hot. Use stainless steel for the cables, the clamps, and the shackles, because they hang above the coals.

Keep 10 feet from anything that burns. The International Fire Code, section 308.1.4, forbids open-flame cooking devices on combustible balconies and within 10 feet of combustible construction (source 14). The code exempts one- and two-family houses, but sparks leave an open fire anyway. Ask the local fire department before you build a fixed masonry pit.

5. Wrong idea corrected: half the height gives 2 times the heat, not 4 times

Common wrong idea

Wrong: "Radiant heat follows the inverse-square law. So a grate at 12 inches gets 4 times the heat of a grate at 24 inches, and at 4 inches 36 times."

Correct: above a 36 × 24 inch coal bed, the grate at 12 inches gets 2.0 times the heat of 24 inches. At 4 inches it gets 3.0 times.

Why the wrong idea fails: the inverse-square law describes a source that is small compared with the distance. A coal bed of 36 × 24 inches is not small at 4 to 24 inches. Close to the bed, the grate sees coals in every direction, and the view factor approaches its maximum of 1. The two curves agree only far from the bed: at 240 inches, the exact view factor is 0.00475 and the point-source value is 0.00477.

6. Limits: what this page did not measure

Limits
  • Nobody built this exact design for this page. A script checks the clearances between the parts, but no test fire took place.
  • The hand wheel is at 69 in. If that is too high for you, lower the firebox floor. Every part above the floor then moves down by the same amount.
  • The heat calculation covers radiation only. Hot air adds heat, and wind removes it. The 700 °C of the coal bed is an example value. The ratios between heights depend on geometry only.
  • The firebrick weight is a forum report. Ask your supplier.
  • Codes differ by place. The 10-foot rule comes from a city document about the 2012 code.

7. More detail: the view factor is 0.314 at 24 in and 0.635 at 12 in

Section 3 states two heat values: 14.4 kW/m² at 24 in and 29.1 kW/m² at 12 in. This section shows each step that leads to them.

The Stefan-Boltzmann law in full: a hot surface sends out ε × σ × T⁴ watts per square metre. T is the absolute temperature in kelvin, and σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴). The factor ε is the emissivity (how well a surface radiates, from 0 to 1; coals are close to 0.9).

Worked example: the heat at the centre of the grate, at 24 in and at 12 in
  1. Coal bed: 36 × 24 in, at 700 °C = 973 K, emissivity 0.9. Power sent out: 0.9 × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 973⁴ = 45.8 kW/m². 700 °C is an example value. A black body at 650 °C looks dark red, and at 760 °C bright red (source 8), like cooking coals.
  2. View factor from a point above the centre of a rectangle: split the rectangle into 4 equal quarters, each with the point above one corner. For one quarter with sides a = 18 in and b = 12 in at height c, use this formula. 
    A = a / c,  B = b / c
F_quarter = (1 / 2π) × [ A / √(1 + A²) × atan( B / √(1 + A²) )
                        + B / √(1 + B²) × atan( A / √(1 + B²) ) ]
F = 4 × F_quarter
    This is configuration B-3 in the Howell catalog of view factors (source 7). A numerical integration on a 400 × 400 grid gives the same 0.6351 at 12 in.
  3. At c = 24 in: A = 0.75, B = 0.5. F = 0.314. Heat at the grate = 45.8 × 0.314 = 14.4 kW/m².
  4. At c = 12 in: A = 1.5, B = 1.0. F = 0.635. Heat = 45.8 × 0.635 = 29.1 kW/m².
  5. Ratio: 29.1 ÷ 14.4 = 2.03. Half the height gives about 2 times the heat. At 12 in the point already sees the coal bed under a wide angle. Lower still, the bed fills almost the whole view, and F cannot exceed 1. At 4 in, F = 0.937: 3.0 times the heat at 24 in.

8. More detail: steel cart or masonry pit

The steel design above is a cart. The traditional Santa Maria grill is a pit: a box of concrete block or brick with the lift frame set on top. The two cases differ in one main feature: where the firebox is.

Steel cart (sections 2 and 4)

The firebox is a welded steel box on a steel frame.

You can move it. It needs about 17 sq ft of sheet and many hours of welding. The steel walls lose heat to the air, so the coals burn down faster.

Masonry pit

The firebox is a pit of concrete block, lined with firebrick, on a concrete slab.

It cannot move, and it needs a slab. The mason work replaces most of the welding. The thick walls store heat, so the coal bed stays even for hours.

In a masonry pit, the firebrick needs mortar. Use refractory mortar (mortar that keeps its strength at the temperature of a fire). The International Residential Code and the standard NFPA 211 require refractory mortar tested to ASTM C199, medium duty, for firebrick (source 15). Lay the brick with joints of 1/16 to 1/8 in. Ordinary cement mortar cracks and falls out of the joints after a few fires.

The lift frame for a pit is the top part of the steel frame in section 4. Two posts of 2 × 2 in tube stand in the block cores, anchored with concrete. The shaft, the drums, the cables, and the wheel are the same.

9. More detail: dense wood gives more heat per cord, but the same heat per pound

The traditional fuel is coast live oak. The university tables on this page do not list coast live oak. They list the eastern red oak, which is a different species. The tables still answer the practical question: what changes when you change the wood?

A cord is a stack of firewood of 4 × 4 × 8 feet, which is 128 cubic feet with the air between the pieces (source 18). The table lists the weight and the heat of one seasoned cord. The last column is my calculation: heat divided by weight.

Per cord, red oak gives 28% more heat than soft maple. Per pound, the two woods give the same heat.

How to read this table: rows are sorted by heat per cord. The last column is almost constant: 6,388 to 6,410 Btu/lb. A second table, from Utah State University, gives 24.6 million Btu and 3,528 lb for a dry cord of red oak (source 19). That is 6,973 Btu/lb. The two tables differ by 9% per pound, because they define a seasoned cord differently. Inside each table, the heat per pound is the same for every wood.

The consequence for the grill: more pounds of a dense wood like oak fit in the firebox. So the coal bed from one load of oak lasts longer than the coal bed from one load of a light wood. A light wood gives the same heat per pound but burns to ash faster. 20 lb of oak release about 128,000 Btu, which is 37.5 kWh. Red oak gives the wood smoke flavour of the Santa Maria tradition. The heat per pound is not the reason to choose it.

10. More detail: words that you will hear

These terms belong to the world of Santa Maria barbecue and open-fire grills. They are not needed for the build, but you will meet them in listings, videos, and forums.

Argentine grill, parrilla
The South American cousin of the Santa Maria grill. It has the same adjustable grate, often with V-shaped channel bars that drain the fat. It often has a brasero, a basket at the side where the wood burns down before the coals are raked under the grate. Many listings use "Santa Maria" and "Argentine" for the same product.
Cantilever arms
A lift design where the grate hangs from two arms that reach over the firebox from the back. No post stands at the front. Yoder uses this design (source 5). The design on this page uses two posts and two cables.
Chef wheel, flywheel, crank wheel
Three names for the hand wheel of the lift. A flywheel in the mechanical sense stores energy; on a grill the word only means a large wheel.
Coast live oak, "red oak"
Quercus agrifolia, an evergreen oak native to the California coast from Mendocino County to northern Baja California (source 20). In the Santa Maria Valley people call it red oak. It is a different species from the eastern red oak, Quercus rubra, in the firewood tables.
Gauge
A number for the thickness of sheet steel. A larger number means a thinner sheet. 11 gauge is 0.1196 in (source 21). Plate thicker than about 3/16 in is named by its thickness, not by a gauge.
Santa Maria-style barbecue
The whole meal, not only the grill: beef over coast live oak coals, with side dishes. Tri-tip joined the tradition in the 1950s (source 1). The Santa Maria Valley Chamber of Commerce holds the copyright of the term.
Tri-tip
A triangular cut from the bottom sirloin of beef. It became the typical cut of the Santa Maria grill after a local butcher, Bob Schutz, prepared it in the 1950s (source 1). It is the reason for the grate size in section 2.

11. Check your understanding

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. You keep the 14 in wheel and the 59 lb load, but you use drums of 3 in diameter. What hand force do you need?
    Show the answer

    About 12.6 lb.

    The drum radius is now 1.5 in. Torque = 59 × 1.5 = 88.5 lb·in. Force at the rim = 88.5 ÷ 7 = 12.6 lb. If your answer was 8.4 lb, you kept the 1 in radius from section 2.

  2. You lower the grate from 16 in to 8 in. By what factor does the radiant heat rise? Compare with the inverse-square prediction.
    Show the answer

    About 1.6 times. The inverse-square rule predicts 4 times.

    From the table in section 3: 36.3 ÷ 22.9 = 1.59. The inverse-square rule gives (16 ÷ 8)² = 4. If you answered 2 times, you remembered the 24-to-12 case.

  3. You want lift arms of 12 in, not 8 in. How high must the shaft be?
    Show the answer

    73 in.

    The highest grate position is 53.5 in. Add the 12 in arm, 6.5 in for the shackle and the clamps, and the 1 in drum radius: 53.5 + 12 + 6.5 + 1 = 73 in. If you answered 69 in, you kept the 8 in arm.

12. Recap, glossary, and sources

Recap
  1. Four parts: firebox with a firebrick floor, frame, heavy grate, lift. The grate height is the only heat control.
  2. Size the firebox from the brick: 40.5 × 27 in inside takes 27 full bricks without a cut. The grate travel then sets the shaft height: 69 in.
  3. The lift carries about 59 lb. The 14 in wheel needs about 8 lb of hand force.
  4. Above a large coal bed, half the height gives about 2 times the radiant heat, not 4 times.
  5. Never weld galvanized steel, and keep the fire 10 feet from anything that burns.

Glossary

Coal bed
The layer of glowing wood coals, 2 to 4 inches deep, over which the food cooks.
Cooking grate (grate)
The flat grid of steel bars that holds the food.
Cord
A stack of firewood of 4 × 4 × 8 feet, 128 cubic feet with the air between the pieces.
Emissivity
How well a surface radiates, on a scale from 0 to 1. Glowing coals are close to 0.9.
Firebox
The open steel box or brick pit that holds the wood, the coals, and the ash.
Hand test
A measure of the heat at the grate: the seconds that you can hold your palm 5 inches above the grate. 2 to 4 seconds is high heat, 8 to 10 seconds is low heat.
Lift
The parts that raise the grate: a shaft, two cable drums, two cables, and a hand wheel with a ratchet.
Minimum breaking strength
The smallest force at which a new cable breaks in a test. For 1/8 in 7×19 stainless steel cable: 1,760 lb.
Pawl
A small lever that drops between the teeth of a ratchet and blocks them in one direction.
Ratchet
A toothed wheel that, together with a pawl, turns in one direction only.
Refractory mortar
Mortar that keeps its strength at the temperature of a fire. The required grade for firebrick is ASTM C199, medium duty.
Santa Maria grill
An open wood-fire grill with no lid, whose cooking grate is raised and lowered over the coals with a hand wheel.
Stefan-Boltzmann law
A surface at absolute temperature T radiates ε × σ × T⁴ watts per square metre, with σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴).
Thermal radiation
Infrared light that carries heat through the air without contact.
View factor
The fraction of the radiation that leaves one surface and arrives at another. It depends only on the geometry.
