What does the book The ONE Thing teach, and how do you apply it?

Written 2026-10-08 · Main path: about 25 minutes · Complete page: about 33 minutes · You need to know: nothing special

The short answer

The book says that extraordinary results come from one action at a time, done in sequence. Each result makes a larger next action possible. The tool is the Focusing Question. The words of the book: What’s the ONE Thing I can do such that by doing it everything else will be easier or unnecessary? You ask it for the far future (your someday goal) and for right now, and you reserve 4 hours a day for the answer.

The model of the book is a chain of dominoes in which each domino is 1.5 times taller than the one before. From a first domino of 5 cm, domino 57 would be 369,000 km tall, almost the distance to the Moon (384,000 km). A new habit took 66 days on average in the study that the book cites, with a range of 18 to 254 days. Two findings behind the claims of the book about willpower were later challenged (part B, limits).

  1. Recap, glossary, and sources

Part A: the idea

The short answer for part A

The book claims that extraordinary success is sequential, not simultaneous. You find the one action that makes the rest easier or unnecessary, and you do that action first. The book calls the chain of actions the domino effect, and it uses the Focusing Question to find the first domino.

1. One example: a chain of dominoes, each 1.5 times taller than the one before

In 1983, Lorne Whitehead published a one-page note in the American Journal of Physics. His point: a falling domino can make a domino fall that is up to about 1.5 times as large in each dimension. The book builds its model on this fact (chapter 2, The Domino Effect, page 13).

The book then imagines a chain that starts with a 2-inch domino, each domino 50% larger than the one before. Such a chain is a geometric progression. In a geometric progression, each number is the number before it multiplied by a fixed ratio. Here the ratio is 1.5.

Worked example: the heights in the chain of the book
  1. Domino 1 is 2 inches tall, which is 5.08 cm. The book starts with a 2-inch domino. One inch is 2.54 cm.
  2. Domino 2 is 5.08 cm × 1.5 = 7.62 cm. Each domino is 1.5 times taller than the one before. This factor is the ratio of the chain.
  3. Domino 10 is 5.08 cm × 1.59 = 195 cm. From domino 1 to domino 10 there are 9 multiplications, so the exponent is 9. The book compares domino 10 with the football player Peyton Manning, who is 196 cm tall.
  4. Domino 31 is 5.08 cm × 1.530 = 9,741 m. Mount Everest is 8,849 m high. The difference is 892 m, or 2,927 feet. The book says almost 3,000 feet.
  5. Domino 57 is 5.08 cm × 1.556 = 369,000 km. The mean distance from the Earth to the Moon is 384,399 km. Domino 57 reaches 96% of that distance.

Result: 56 multiplications by 1.5 turn 5 cm into 369,000 km.

The general formula, and the same formula with the numbers of domino 57:

height of domino n = height of domino 1 × ratio^(n − 1)
369,000 km         = 5.08 cm           × 1.5^56

What the dominoes stand for, and where the comparison stops

The book uses the chain as a model of work. One domino is one action, and the height of the domino is the size of its result. The ratio 1.5 is the claim that each finished action makes a somewhat larger action possible. The first domino is the lead domino, the action that starts the chain.

The comparison stops at two points. The ratio 1.5 comes from the energy of a falling block; nobody has measured such a ratio for actions. And the model gives no number for how often an action fails and stops the chain.

Guess first

Which domino is the first one taller than the Eiffel Tower (330 m)? Choose before you continue: domino 12, 23, or 45.

Answer to the guess: domino 23. Domino 22 is 253 m tall, below the tower. Domino 23 is 380 m tall, 15% above the tower.

Ratio 1.0, the dashed line

Every domino stays 5.08 cm. In the model of the book: a list of equal tasks, where each finished task leaves the next task the same size.

Ratio 1.5, the solid line

Domino 13 is already 6.59 m, 130 times the first. In the model of the book: each finished action makes a larger action possible.

The two chains differ in one feature only, the ratio. The book says: What starts out linear becomes geometric. So the claim of the book is a claim about the ratio: choose actions that make the next action larger, and do them in order.

2. The rule: the Focusing Question finds the first domino, and the Pareto principle shortens the list

The book gives one question to find the first domino. It calls the question the Focusing Question: the question that selects the single action with the largest effect on everything else. The words of the book:

“What’s the ONE Thing I can do such that by doing it everything else will be easier or unnecessary?”

The book explains the three parts of the question (chapter 10, from page 102). Part one, What’s the ONE Thing I can do, asks for one action, not a wish and not a list. Part two, such that by doing it, asks for a consequence: the action must change what comes after it. Part three, everything else will be easier or unnecessary, is the test of the answer. The answer is the ONE Thing: the single action that passes the test at this moment, for this area of your life.

The question comes in two forms. The big-picture form, What’s my ONE Thing?, sets the direction for the long term. The small-focus form, What’s my ONE Thing right now?, chooses the next action, and the book says to ask it throughout the day.

The Pareto principle turns a to-do list into a success list

Which action passes the test? The book answers with the Pareto principle: the observation that a small share of the causes produces a large share of the results. Vilfredo Pareto found that 80% of the land in Italy belonged to 20% of the people, and Joseph Juran later named the pattern after him.

A to-do list holds everything that you could do. A success list is the same list after you apply the Pareto principle: you keep the few items that produce most of the result. The book says: A to-do list becomes a success list when you apply Pareto’s Principle to it. Then the book calls for a further step, extreme Pareto: take 20% of the 20%, and repeat until one item remains. With 10 items, the first step keeps 2 and the second step keeps 1. That item is the ONE Thing, at the top of the success list.

3. Wrong idea corrected: the book does not say “do only one thing in your life”

Common wrong idea

Wrong: “The ONE Thing means: pick one goal, and ignore everything else.”

Correct: the book asks the Focusing Question separately for each area of life and for each time scale. The areas in the book include the job, the family and the spiritual life (chapter 11, page 112). The ONE Thing is one action at a time, inside one area, not one goal forever.

Why the wrong idea fails: the question itself contains the words everything else. The other tasks do not disappear; they become easier or unnecessary. The book reserves 4 hours of the day for the ONE Thing, not the whole day. The other areas of life get their own time, see section 4.

4. More detail: the book names six beliefs that stop people, and calls them lies

Part one of the book, The Lies, names six lies, one chapter each. Each chapter names a common belief about success and argues that the belief is false. Four of the chapters need a term.

Multitasking is the attempt to do two tasks at the same time. The book says that the brain switches between the tasks instead, and each switch costs time. The book quotes the Stanford professor Clifford Nass on people who multitask heavily: Multitaskers were just lousy at everything.

The book adds two numbers. The first: Researchers estimate we lose 28 percent of an average workday to multitasking ineffectiveness. The second: workers are interrupted every 11 minutes. The 28% figure matches a 2005 report by the consultancy Basex, which estimated 2.1 hours lost per day. The book does not name the source.

Willpower is the ability to make yourself do what you decided to do. The book treats willpower as a supply: We act as though our supply of willpower were endless. The evidence in the book is a study of 1,112 parole hearings in Israel. The judges granted parole in about 65% of the cases after a break, and in almost 0% before the next break. The book concludes: do the ONE Thing early in the day, when willpower is high. Section 8 shows why this study is disputed.

Counterbalancing is the replacement of the book for balance. You give the ONE Thing most of your time, you let other areas wait, and you return to them before the damage is large. The book says: Counterbalancing done well gives the illusion of balance. The book names two types: between work and personal life, and within each of the two.

A habit is a behavior that you perform without a decision, because you performed it many times in the same situation. The answer of the book to the lie of discipline is: Build one habit at a time. Success is sequential, not simultaneous.

The split is not always 80 to 20. The book says: 90 percent of your success comes from 20 percent of your effort. Or 70/10 or 65/5. The exact numbers do not matter. The shape matters: a few items carry most of the result.

5. Check your understanding of part A

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. Domino 1 is 5.08 cm tall, and the ratio is 1.5. How tall is domino 15?
    Show the answer

    About 14.8 m.

    From domino 1 to domino 15 there are 14 multiplications: 5.08 cm × 1.514 = 1,483 cm. If your answer was 22.2 m, you used the exponent 15. If it was about 1.1 m, you added instead of multiplied. See section 1.

  2. Your to-do list has 10 items. How does the book turn the list into a success list, and what goes at the top?
    Show the answer

    Apply the Pareto principle: keep the 20% of the items that produce most of the result, which is 2 items. Apply it again, and 1 item remains: the ONE Thing, at the top of the success list. A common wrong answer sorts all 10 items by importance and works through them in order. See section 2.

Part B: the method

The short answer for part B

The method has four steps. First, goal setting to the now: a chain of seven Focusing Questions from your someday goal to right now. Second, a time block: 4 hours a day for the ONE Thing, after your time off and before your planning time. Third, repetition until the block is a habit, about 66 days on average. Fourth, protection of the block against the four thieves (section 9).

6. One example: from a someday goal to the next four hours

The book calls the chain of questions goal setting to the now (page 150). It is a sequence of seven questions, and each question starts from the answer of the one before it. The book compares the chain with a Russian matryoshka doll. The ONE Thing today is nested inside the ONE Thing this week, and so on.

The example below uses example values: a person who wants to earn a living as an author. The plan has 48 writing weeks (4 weeks off) and 5 writing days per week, which gives 240 writing days.

Worked example: the seven questions with example answers
  1. Someday goal: earn a living as an author. The first question asks what you want to do some day. The answer has no number; it gives the direction.
  2. Five-year goal: publish two books. The question of the book: Based on my someday goal, what’s the ONE Thing I can do in the next five years? Two published books are a concrete step toward a living as an author.
  3. One-year goal: finish a manuscript of 60,000 words and send it to publishers. A finished manuscript makes the next steps possible: an agent, a publisher. This is the domino effect of section 1.
  4. This month: write 5,000 words. 60,000 words ÷ 12 months = 5,000 words per month.
  5. This week: write 1,250 words. 5,000 words ÷ 4 writing weeks = 1,250 words per week.
  6. Today: write 250 words in the 4 reserved hours. 1,250 words ÷ 5 writing days = 250 words per day.
  7. Right now: open the file and write the first sentence of the next scene. The last question is the small-focus question: What’s my ONE Thing right now? The answer is an action that you can start in the next minute.

Result: the chain turns a direction without a number (an author) into one action for the next minute (one sentence).

250 new words per day is a slow pace, on purpose: the 4 hours also hold research and revision. With 1,000 new words per day, the draft takes 60 writing days.

7. The rule: block four hours a day, in a fixed order, and repeat for about 66 days

Time blocking is the practice of the book for the daily ONE Thing. You reserve a fixed period in your calendar and treat it like an appointment that nobody can move. The reserved period is the time block.

The book gives the length in chapter 15, page 156: block four hours a day. This isn’t a typo. I repeat: four hours a day. That is half of an 8-hour workday.

The book says to block three things, in this order: first your time off, then your ONE Thing, then your planning time. Rest comes first so that the time block does not consume it. In the planning time you set the next time blocks.

The example of the book is the writer Stephen King. The book asks: does he get to do this because he is Stephen King, or is he Stephen King because he does this?

A habit took 66 days on average in the study that the book cites

The time block must become a habit, a behavior that you perform without a decision. The book says: Research says this will take about 66 days. The research is a 2010 study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London. The numbers:

  • 96 volunteers chose one daily behavior, for example a glass of water after breakfast, and reported each day for 84 days.
  • The time to reach 95% of the final level of automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days. The average was 66 days.
  • About half of the participants had not reached their final level by day 84, so the 254 days is a prediction of the model.

So 66 days is an average with a wide range, not a fixed duration. The book reports the range too: The full range was 18 to 254 days. Section 9 names the four things that, in the book, take the time block away: the four thieves.

8. Limits: three sources of the book were later challenged, and the method itself has no controlled test

Limits
  • The method as a whole was not tested in a controlled study. The book supports it with examples of successful people, and examples cannot show how many people used the method and did not succeed.
  • Four hours a day needs control over your calendar. A person with fixed shifts, or with meetings all day, cannot block 4 hours without a change of the job.
  • The ratio 1.5 is physics. An idealized model from 2013 puts the maximum ratio near 2. Nobody measured such a ratio for actions.
  • The science in part one of the book is uneven. The table below lists the sources and what happened later.

9. More detail: the four thieves, and other words that you will hear

These terms belong to the book, but not to the main path of this page. You will meet them in summaries, in the workbooks of the authors and on their website.

  • The four thieves (chapter 17, page 190): the four things that the book says take the time block. First, the inability to say no: every yes to another request is a no to the ONE Thing. Second, the fear of chaos: when you give 4 hours to the ONE Thing, other things stay undone. The book says to accept the mess. Third, poor health habits: the block needs energy, and the book argues that the energy comes from your health. Fourth, an environment that does not support your goals: the people and the places around you must support the goal.
  • The success habit (chapter 11, page 112): the name of the book for the daily use of the Focusing Question. It is the habit that takes about 66 days to build, see section 7.
  • Big and specific (chapter 12, page 119): the form of a good question. A great question is big, because it aims at extraordinary results, and specific, because you can check whether you reached the result. The one-year goal of section 6, 60,000 words within 12 months, is big and specific.
  • The three commitments (chapter 16, page 175). First, follow the path of mastery: treat the ONE Thing as a skill with no final level. Second, move from E to P. E is the entrepreneurial approach, which does what comes naturally. P is the purposeful approach, which looks for a better method when the results stop. Third, live the accountability cycle: own the results, with an accountability partner if possible. These are attitudes for the time block, not extra steps.
  • The six lies. Section 4 explains them.

10. Check your understanding of part B

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. Your one-year goal is to run 1,000 km. Use the plan of section 6 (48 active weeks, 5 active days per week). What are the goals for this month, this week and today?
    Show the answer

    83 km this month, 21 km this week, 4.2 km today.

    1,000 km ÷ 12 months = 83.3 km per month. 83.3 km ÷ 4 active weeks = 20.8 km per week. 20.8 km ÷ 5 active days = 4.2 km per day. A common wrong answer divides 1,000 km by 365 days (2.7 km per day), which assumes a run on every day of the year. See section 6.

  2. A colleague says: “The book proves that willpower runs out before lunch. The study of the judges shows it.” What is the state of the evidence?
    Show the answer

    The study found the drop. But a later analysis found that the order of the cases was not random: prisoners without a lawyer came last and won less often. A 2016 simulation explains part of the drop by judges who avoid a long case before a break. So the claim is disputed, not proven. See section 8.

  3. Is 66 days the time that a habit takes?
    Show the answer

    No. 66 days is the average in one study of 96 people. The range was 18 to 254 days, and about half of the participants had not reached their final level by day 84. See section 7.

11. Recap, glossary, and sources

Recap
  1. The claim of the book: success is sequential. Find the one action that makes everything else easier or unnecessary, and do it first.
  2. The model is a geometric progression. With the ratio 1.5, 56 steps turn 5 cm into 369,000 km. The ratio comes from physics, not from work.
  3. The Focusing Question has three parts: one action, a consequence, and the test “easier or unnecessary”. The Pareto principle shortens the list to that action.
  4. The method: seven questions from some day to right now, then 4 reserved hours a day, repeated for about 66 days. Block time off first, then the ONE Thing, then planning.
  5. The evidence of the book on willpower is disputed, and the ego depletion effect did not replicate in 23 laboratories. The method itself has no controlled test.
