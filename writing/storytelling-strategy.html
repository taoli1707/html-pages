What is a good storytelling strategy, and how do you apply it to a true story about a company?

Written 2026-10-11 · Reading time: about 38 minutes for the whole page, about 25 minutes for the main path · You need to know: nothing about writing. The data section uses two statistics, and the page explains both.

The short answer

A good strategy has three parts. Structure: one person, one routine, one event that breaks the routine, a chain of consequences, one turning point, and a new routine. The story spine of Kenn Adams (1991) gives this structure in eight lines. Links: join every two events with "therefore" or "but", never with "and then". Shape: the fortune of the person must move, and the reader must see it move.

The evidence that this matters is measured. In 37 studies with 33,078 participants, readers understood and recalled a story better than an essay with the same content. The advantage was 0.55 standard deviations (Mar and others, 2021). Stories also moved beliefs and behaviour, but only a little: correlations of 0.17 to 0.23 (Braddock and Dillard, 2016).

In 1,327 novels, six basic shapes of fortune appeared. The three shapes with the most downloads were the ones with a fall: "Icarus", "Oedipus" and "two men in a hole" (Reagan and others, 2016). So a true story should not hide its bad moments. The bad moments are the parts that readers come for.

The example on this page is the public story of Base44. One person built that company and sold it to Wix for US$80 million in 2025. The same facts appear two times: as a list, and as a story.

Assumptions on this page
  • You want to tell true stories: about a company, a product, or a project. You cannot invent events. The strategy chooses the start, the end, and the links between the facts.
  • Your stories are short: 300 to 1,500 words. The page says in section 4 which structure fits a longer text.
  • The Base44 facts come from your own notes about Base44, with the sources that those notes name. This page did not verify them again.

1. One example: the Base44 facts as a list, and as a story in eight lines

Here are the facts of Base44 in time order. Maor Shlomo, age 31, had founded the data company Explorium in 2017. At the end of 2024 he started Base44 as a side project. Base44 is a tool: you describe an app in plain language, and the tool builds the app with its database, user login, and hosting.

He launched it in January 2025. It had 10,000 users after three weeks. In May 2025 it made a profit of US$189,000. On 18 June 2025, Wix bought it for about US$80 million in cash. By May 2026, Base44 made subscription revenue of US$150 million a year.

This list is correct and complete. It is also not a story. A story (also called a narrative) is a text in which one event causes the next event, and a person changes because of the events. The list above is exposition (also called expository text), which is a text that states facts and explains them. The difference is not the facts. The difference is the links between the facts, and the person at the centre.

The person at the centre is the protagonist: the one person whose fortune the story follows from the first line to the last line. In the Base44 story, the protagonist is Shlomo. Base44 is a product, and Wix is a company. Neither of them can want something or lose something. Shlomo can.

The eight lines below use the story spine. The story spine is a template of eight sentence starts, created by the improvisation teacher Kenn Adams in 1991. Each line has one job. The worked example fills each line with Base44 facts and gives the reason for each choice.

Worked example: the Base44 story on the story spine
  1. Once upon a time there was an engineer, Maor Shlomo, who had already founded one company that raised about US$125 million. Line 1 names the protagonist and the starting state. The reader needs a person before the reader needs a product.
  2. Every day, people who could not program paid companies such as Builder.ai US$20,000 for apps that cost US$35,000 to make. Line 2 is the routine: the world as it was, with a problem that nobody had solved. Builder.ai lost US$15,000 on each app.
  3. But one day, at the end of 2024, AI (artificial intelligence) models became able to write a whole app correctly. Line 3 is the complication: the one event that breaks the routine and creates the problem of the story. The story needs exactly one such event. Without it, nothing that follows has a reason.
  4. Because of that, Shlomo did not return to his old company and built Base44 alone, as a side project. Line 4 is the first consequence. The word "because" forces you to show the cause. Here the cause is the new ability of the models.
  5. Because of that, Base44 hid the server, the database and the login from the user. 10,000 people used it within three weeks of the launch in January 2025. Line 5 is the second consequence. Competitors such as Lovable and Bolt gave users code that they still had to deploy. Base44 did not.
  6. Because of that, the company earned money from the first day, and in May 2025 it made a profit of US$189,000 in one month. Line 6 is the third consequence. The numbers show the rise. A rise without numbers is a claim.
  7. Until finally, on 18 June 2025, Wix bought Base44 for about US$80 million in cash, five months after the launch. Line 7 is the turning point: the event after which the protagonist cannot return to the routine of line 2.
  8. And ever since then, Base44 has grown inside Wix to US$150 million of subscription revenue a year. The next question is whether a single person can repeat this. Line 8 is the new routine. It states the change, and it tells the reader what the story means for the reader.

Result: the same facts, but now every line answers the question "why did this happen?". The reader can predict and can be surprised. The list in the first paragraph allows neither.

The spine is not the text that you publish. It is the plan of the text. The published paragraph below comes from the spine. It has 123 words, and every sentence after the second one begins with a cause or a contrast.

Maor Shlomo had already built one company. At the end of 2024, AI models crossed a threshold: a generated app ran on the first try. So Shlomo built Base44 alone, as a side project, for people who cannot program. Competitors gave their users code that still had to be deployed. Base44 hid the server, the database and the login. Therefore 10,000 people used it in the first three weeks after the launch in January 2025, and they paid from the first day. In May 2025 the one-person company made US$189,000 of profit. Five months after the launch, on 18 June 2025, Wix bought it for about US$80 million in cash. Inside Wix, it reached US$150 million of yearly subscription revenue by May 2026.

The links: "therefore" and "but", never "and then"

The second part of the strategy is a test for the links between events. Trey Parker and Matt Stone write the television series South Park. They described the test in a class at New York University (NYU) in 2011.

They write the events of an episode on a board. Then they read the words between every two events. If the words are "and then", the episode has a problem. The words must be "therefore" or "but".

This page calls the test the but-therefore rule. Every two consecutive events in a story are joined by a cause ("therefore") or by a contrast ("but"). "And then" joins two events only in time. "Therefore" joins them by cause. "But" joins them by a blocked expectation. Figure 2 applies the rule to the Base44 facts.

Chain B contains one "but". A "but" is a blocked expectation: the reader expects that competitors with more money win, but Base44 hid the server and they did not. Each "but" is a small fall in the fortune of the protagonist. Each "therefore" is a step up or a step down that the reader can predict. A story needs both. A chain of only "therefore" is a proof, and a proof has no surprise.

The shape: the fortune must move, and the reader must see it move

The third part of the strategy is the emotional arc. The emotional arc is the curve of the fortune of the protagonist from the start of the story to the end. The writer Kurt Vonnegut described in a book of 1981 his idea that stories have a small number of such curves. He drew a story on two axes: time from beginning to end, and fortune from ill to great. Section 3 shows what a measurement of 1,327 novels found.

For a true story, the facts fix the direction of the curve. You cannot make a failure into a success. But you choose two things. You choose the start. A story that starts in a bad routine has a different arc from a story that starts in a good one. And you choose which "but" events you keep, because each "but" is a visible fall.

The Base44 story in section 1 starts in the bad routine of Builder.ai, so the curve starts low and rises. The same facts, started at the launch, give a flat curve that only rises at the sale.

3. More detail: stories with a fall are downloaded most, and stories are recalled 0.55 standard deviations better

Guess first

The study below measured 1,327 novels. Which shape of fortune was the most common: a rise, a fall, or a fall followed by a rise? Choose one before you continue.

Andrew Reagan and four colleagues at the University of Vermont measured the emotional arc of 1,327 English novels from the free library Project Gutenberg. For each novel, they scored the words of each part of the text with a dictionary of word happiness. The curve of these scores along the text is the emotional arc of the novel. Then they searched for the basic shapes from which all the curves are built. They found six.

The study used only books with 20,000 to 100,000 words and more than 40 downloads. Figure 3 shows the six shapes, plus one combined shape, with the share of the novels and the downloads.

The study measured success with the number of downloads of each novel from Project Gutenberg. The figure shows the median: the middle value, with half of the novels above it and half below it. The study reports the mean too, but the authors write that a few novels with very many downloads make the mean unreliable. This page follows their advice and uses the median.

Answer to the guess: the most common shape was a fall. "Tragedy" was the closest shape for 407 of the 1,327 novels (30.7%). A plain rise, "rags to riches", was the closest shape for 133 novels (10.0%). A fall then a rise, "man in a hole", was the closest shape for 148 novels (11.2%).

The result that matters for your strategy is in the last line of each panel. The novels nearest to the "man in a hole" shape had a median of 76 downloads. The novels with two such dips, "two men in a hole", had a median of 105.5 downloads. That is 1.39 times the first value.

The authors name Icarus, Oedipus and the two men in a hole as the three most successful arcs. All three contain a fall that the reader sees. A story in which the protagonist only rises was not among the three.

Stories are recalled and understood better than essays: 0.55 standard deviations

Why does the structure matter for a reader? Raymond Mar and three colleagues at York University collected every experiment of one kind. In these experiments, people read a story or an essay with the same content, and then answered questions. This is a meta-analysis: a study that combines the results of many earlier studies into one estimate. They found 37 articles with 150 comparisons, 78 samples of readers, and 33,078 participants in total.

The result is given as Hedges g: the difference between two groups, divided by the standard deviation of the scores. A g of 1 means that the two groups differ by one standard deviation. Here is the same number in a concrete form.

Suppose that the readers of an essay recall 40 of 100 facts, with a standard deviation of 15 facts. A g of 0.55 means that the readers of the story version recall 40 + 0.55 × 15 = 48.25 facts. For memory tests only, g was 0.72, so the story readers recall 40 + 0.72 × 15 = 50.8 facts. Figure 4 shows the three values with their confidence intervals.

A second meta-analysis asked a different question: does a story change what the reader believes and does? Kurt Braddock and James Dillard combined studies in which one group read a story and another group did not. The result is given as a correlation coefficient r. This is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how strongly the story and the outcome move together. Jacob Cohen proposed in 1992 that r = 0.10 is a small effect, 0.30 a medium effect, and 0.50 a large effect. The table shows the four results.

One more number is often quoted, and it needs a warning. In the book "Made to Stick" (2007), Chip Heath describes an exercise in his class at Stanford. Students give a one-minute speech. The average speech uses 2.5 statistics, and only one student in ten tells a story.

Afterwards, 63% of the listeners remember the stories, and 5% remember any single statistic. This is a class exercise, not a controlled experiment, and the book gives no sample size. So the 63% and the 5% are an illustration, not evidence of the same quality as the two meta-analyses.

4. More detail: Base44 against Builder.ai: the same promise, two shapes, and which structure fits which text

Builder.ai promised the same thing as Base44: apps with no programmer. It raised US$445 million from 2016 to 2025. It sold apps for US$20,000 that cost US$35,000 to make, and people did the work behind the AI label. It collapsed in May 2025, one month before Wix bought Base44. The two stories have the same spine and opposite shapes.

Base44: fall, then rise

Routine: apps for non-programmers lose money. Complication: AI models can write a whole app. Chain: one person builds the all-in-one version, 10,000 users, profit. Turning point: the sale for US$80 million.

Shape: the story starts low, in the routine of Builder.ai, and ends high. This is a "man in a hole" arc.

Builder.ai: rise, then fall

Routine: a company sells the promise of AI-built apps. Complication: each app costs US$15,000 more than its price. Chain: more funding, US$445 million in total, hides the loss for years. Turning point: the collapse in May 2025.

Shape: the story starts high, with funding and press, and ends low. This is an "Icarus" arc.

The principle: the spine is the same for a success and for a failure. The facts decide the direction of the arc. The writer decides the start, the end, and which "but" events the reader sees. The Base44 story becomes stronger when it starts with the failure of Builder.ai, because then the reader sees the hole before the rise. The Builder.ai story becomes stronger when it starts with the funding, because then the reader sees the height before the fall.

Which structure fits which text

The story spine fits a short true story. Other kinds of text use other structures, and the table says which. The last two rows are not stories. They are structures for exposition, and they belong here because people call them "storytelling" too.

Barbara Minto, the author of the SCQA structure, calls it "the classic pattern of story-telling". It still puts the answer first, so it is exposition with a story-shaped opening.

5. Wrong idea corrected: a timeline is not a story

Common wrong idea

Wrong: "A story is the events in the order in which they happened. If I write the timeline well, I have told the story."

Correct: a timeline is exposition. A story needs a protagonist, a complication, causal links, and a turning point. The timeline of Base44 in section 1 has all the facts and none of these four.

Why the wrong idea fails: time order does not tell the reader why an event happened. So the reader cannot predict the next event and cannot be surprised by it. Parker and Stone's board test finds this fault in one minute: a timeline has "and then" between every two events. The memory advantage of 0.55 standard deviations was measured for stories, not for lists of events.

6. Limits: the evidence comes from novels and from reading tests, not from business stories

Limits
  • The arcs come from old novels. The 1,327 novels are English fiction from Project Gutenberg, a library of books whose copyright has expired. Downloads from one website measured success. No study on this page measured the arcs of business stories.
  • The download differences are small in absolute terms. The medians range from 76 to 105.5 downloads. The means are 2.5 to 4.7 times larger, so a few popular novels dominate each group.
  • The 0.55 is an average with large variation. The 150 comparisons differ much from each other. The authors report that 97% of the variation between results is real variation, not chance. Studies with fewer participants found larger effects, which suggests some publication bias.
  • The persuasion effects are small. An r of 0.17 to 0.23 explains 2.9% to 5.3% of the variation in the outcome. The behaviour result rests on 5 studies.
  • The but-therefore rule has no measurement. It comes from a class, described by two secondary sources. This page did not watch the video of the class.
  • The Base44 facts were not verified again. They come from your notes, which grade each number by its source. The user and profit numbers are founder claims.
  • A true story cannot invent a complication. If the facts contain no break and no turning point, the honest text is exposition, and the strategy on this page does not apply.

7. Words that you will hear

These terms belong to the craft of stories, and you will meet them in books and in talks. They are not needed for the strategy above.

Freytag's pyramid
A five-part shape of a drama from Gustav Freytag's book of 1863: introduction, rise, climax, return or fall, catastrophe. The story spine is the same shape with the names of the parts changed and the ending open.
Hero's journey
A pattern of myths that Joseph Campbell described in 1949 and Christopher Vogler adapted for screenwriters in 1992. A hero leaves the ordinary world, passes tests, and returns changed. It is a long form of the spine, with many consequences.
Inciting incident
The screenwriters' name for the complication: the event that starts the problem of the story.
In medias res
A Latin phrase from the poet Horace: a story that starts in the middle of the action and adds the routine later. It moves line 3 of the spine before lines 1 and 2.
Narrative transportation
The state of a reader who is absorbed into the world of a story. Melanie Green and Timothy Brock measured it in 2000. Van Laer and others (2014) combined 132 effect sizes from 76 articles. They treat transportation as the main path from a story to a changed belief.
Nut graf
In journalism, the paragraph after the opening that states what the article is about and why it matters. In a success story, the nut graf often states the result before the story starts.
Show, don't tell
Give the reader an action, a number, or a scene instead of a judgment. "The company made US$189,000 of profit in May" shows. "The company was very successful" tells.
Stakes
What the protagonist loses if the problem is not solved. In the Base44 story, the stakes are low for Shlomo, because he had money from his first company. Low stakes are a weakness of that story.

8. Check your understanding

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. In the Base44 story of section 1, why is the launch in January 2025 not the complication?
    Show the answer

    The complication is the event that breaks the routine and that the protagonist did not control: AI models became able to write a whole app. The launch is a consequence of that event. A typical wrong answer puts the launch in line 3, because it is the first event in the timeline of the company. But a story that starts at the launch has no "because" for the launch.

  2. Rewrite this sentence with the but-therefore rule: "Wix bought Base44, and then Base44 reached US$150 million of revenue a year."
    Show the answer

    One correct version: "Wix had millions of website customers and no app builder. Therefore it bought Base44, and Base44's revenue rose from about US$3.5 million to US$150 million a year." The link is a cause: the sales channel of Wix explains the growth. A version with "but" is also possible: "Base44 was profitable, but it had no sales channel. Therefore Shlomo sold it to Wix." A wrong answer keeps "and then" and only adds adjectives.

  3. A company grows for three years, loses its largest customer, recovers, and then fails. Which emotional arc of Figure 3 is this, and was it among the three most downloaded shapes?
    Show the answer

    Rise, fall, rise, fall. If the story starts at the loss of the customer, the nearest shape in Figure 3 is "Oedipus" (fall, rise, fall). If it starts at the growth, the nearest shape is "Icarus" (rise, fall) with an extra dip. Oedipus and Icarus were both among the three shapes with the highest median downloads (94 and 97). A typical wrong answer is "Cinderella", but Cinderella ends with a rise.

  4. Readers of an essay recall 30 of 50 facts on average, with a standard deviation of 8 facts. Use the overall g of 0.55 to estimate the recall of the story version.
    Show the answer

    30 + 0.55 × 8 = 34.4 facts. The g value is a difference in standard deviations, so you multiply it by the standard deviation of the test, not by the mean. A typical wrong answer multiplies 30 by 1.55 and gets 46.5, which treats g as a percentage.

9. Recap, glossary, and sources

Recap
  1. A story is a chain of causes with one protagonist who changes. A timeline is exposition.
  2. Plan the story on the eight lines of the story spine: routine, one complication, three consequences, one turning point, a new routine.
  3. Test every link between two events: "therefore" or "but" is a story, "and then" is a list.
  4. Let the reader see the falls. In 1,327 novels, the three most downloaded shapes all contained a fall, with medians of 94 to 105.5 against 76 to 89.
  5. The structure pays: readers recalled and understood stories 0.55 standard deviations better than essays. Persuasion effects are small, r = 0.17 to 0.23.

Glossary

But-therefore rule
Every two consecutive events in a story are joined by a cause ("therefore") or by a contrast ("but"), never only by time ("and then").
Complication
The one event that breaks the routine and creates the problem of the story. Line 3 of the story spine.
Correlation coefficient r
A number between 0 and 1 that measures how strongly two things move together. 0.10 is small, 0.30 medium, 0.50 large.
Emotional arc
The curve of the fortune of the protagonist from the start of the story to the end.
Exposition
A text that states facts and explains them, without a protagonist and without causal links between events. Also called expository text.
Hedges g
The difference between two groups divided by the standard deviation of the scores. A g of 0.55 is just over half a standard deviation.
Median
The middle value of a list: half of the values are above it and half are below it.
Meta-analysis
A study that combines the results of many earlier studies into one estimate.
Protagonist
The one person whose fortune the story follows from the first line to the last line.
Story
A text in which one event causes the next event, and a person changes because of the events. Also called a narrative.
Story spine
A template of eight sentence starts by Kenn Adams (1991). The starts are: Once upon a time, Every day, But one day, Because of that (three times), Until finally, And ever since then.
Turning point
The event after which the protagonist cannot return to the old routine. Line 7 of the story spine. Also called the climax.
