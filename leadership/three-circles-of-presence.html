What are the three circles of presence, and how does a leader use them?

Written 2026-09-30 · Reading time: about 23 minutes · You need to know: nothing special. You have sat in a team meeting.

The short answer

The three circles are a model by Patsy Rodenburg, a British voice coach who trained actors for more than 40 years. She published it in 2008 in the book The Second Circle. The model describes where your attention, breath, and voice go when you are with other people. Rodenburg calls this combination "energy".

First circle: the energy goes inward, to yourself. Second circle: the energy goes to the specific person in front of you, and comes back. Third circle: the energy goes outward to everyone in general, with force. Rodenburg's claim is simple: presence is the second circle, and the other two circles are not presence.

For a leader, the practical use is one habit. Before you speak or listen, notice which circle you are in, and move to the second circle. The model comes from theatre training, not from controlled studies. I found no experiment that tests it.

Assumptions on this page
  • You lead a team, or you want to lead one. You met the term "leadership presence" in a training or in feedback from a manager.
  • The word "energy" on this page has only Rodenburg's meaning: attention, breath, and voice, directed somewhere. It is not a physical quantity.
  • The team in the example is invented. The numbers in the example are example values.

1. One example: the same 30-second announcement in three circles

You lead a team of 6 engineers. In the weekly meeting you must announce that the release moves 2 weeks later. The announcement takes about 30 seconds. The team will judge your presence in these 30 seconds. Presence is the state where your attention is on the people in front of you, and they can feel it.

Rodenburg describes this state with one word and three names. The word is energy: your attention, your breath, and your voice, taken together and directed somewhere. The three names are the directions of the energy. In the first circle the energy goes inward, to yourself. In the second circle the energy goes to one specific person and comes back. In the third circle the energy goes outward to everyone in general, with force.

Presence is the second circle. Here is the announcement, done in the second circle, step by step.

Worked example: the announcement in the second circle
  1. Before you speak, put both feet flat on the floor and let your shoulders hang. Breathe out once, slowly. Rodenburg starts every circle from the body. A slumped spine pulls you into the first circle. A rigid, lifted chest pushes you into the third circle. Feet on the floor and free shoulders are her signs of a second-circle body (book, pages 45 to 49).
  2. Look at one person, for example the engineer who owns the release. Keep your eyes on that person. Second-circle energy goes to one specific target and comes back. Eyes that scan the whole room send the energy to everyone in general, which is the third circle.
  3. Say the fact in one sentence: "The release moves from 14 October to 28 October." One clear sentence keeps the energy directed. A long, hedged sentence usually comes from the first circle, where you speak more to yourself than to the listener.
  4. Stop. Wait about 3 seconds. Watch the faces. The second circle is an exchange. The energy must come back before you send more. If you continue at once, you push, and you move into the third circle.
  5. When the first question comes, look at the person who asked, and answer that person. Then look at the next person. Listening is half of the exchange. In the second circle "we listen generously and attentively" (Listening Partnership, on Rodenburg's book).

Result: the team hears one clear fact, and each person who asks a question gets your full attention. This is what Rodenburg means with the sentence "In second circle you are noticed, heard, powerful and remembered."

Now the same announcement in the other two circles. The words can be the same. The direction of the energy is different, and the team feels the difference.

First circle: energy goes inward

You look at your laptop. Your voice is low and drops at the end of the sentence. You add many qualifiers: "So, we probably need to, well, look at moving the date, maybe two weeks."

What the team feels: you are in the room, but not with them. They are not sure what was decided. Some stop asking questions.

Third circle: energy goes outward to everyone

You stand, raise your voice, and scan the room. "Listen up, everyone. The release is moving. Two weeks. No discussion needed, we have a plan." You continue before anyone can react.

What the team feels: pressure. Nobody was addressed personally, so nobody feels heard. Questions stay unspoken.

The two failed versions fail in opposite directions. In the first circle the energy does not reach the team. In the third circle the energy goes past the team. The second circle is the only version where the energy arrives at a person and returns.

2. The rule: the circle is the direction of your energy

Rodenburg says that human energy moves between people in three basic ways, and she draws each way as a circle around you. You are always in one of the three circles when you are with other people, and you can move between them within seconds. Her full definitions are more exact than the short names in section 1.

  • The first circle is the circle of withdrawal. The energy goes inward. You absorb or block the energy of other people, and you draw all outside stimuli into yourself.
  • The second circle is the circle of connection. The energy goes to one specific object of attention, touches it, and the energy that you need comes back. Rodenburg's words for this are "the give and take of being present."
  • The third circle is the circle of force. The energy goes outward, either to nobody in particular or with force at a target, and the motive is control.

Rodenburg is a voice coach, so her descriptions of the circles are physical. The table below collects the signs of each circle from her body checklist and from the coaching sources. The row "What others feel" is the row that matters for a leader.

3. Second circle is the default; first and third have narrow uses

Rodenburg says that we need all three circles and move between them. But she also says that every great performer, and every leader, should live in one circle all the time: the second circle. The first and the third circle have a place, but the place is narrow. The table below is a decision table for a leader.

The second circle fits most leadership moments; the other two fit only rest and emergencies
SituationCircle that fitsWhy
A one-to-one talk, a performance review, a difficult questionSecondThe other person must feel heard before they accept your answer.
A team meeting, an announcement, a decisionSecond, one person at a timeEnergy to the whole room at once becomes the third circle. Address one person, then the next.
A crisis in the first minutes: a production outage, a safety eventThird, for a short timeRodenburg allows the third circle "to respond to a crisis". One loud, general instruction stops the wrong action. Return to the second circle as soon as the instruction is given.
Rest, reflection, thinking alone after a hard dayFirstRodenburg names withdrawal "for reflection, or to rest" as a chosen use of the first circle. Do this alone, not in the meeting.

How to read this table: find the row that matches your situation. The second column gives the circle. The highlighted rows are the normal work of a leader.

Compare the two highlighted rows with the crisis row. In the crisis, you are allowed to push energy outward for one instruction. In the two normal rows, the same push destroys the exchange. The principle is: the circle must match the moment, and in leadership most moments need the exchange of the second circle.

How coaches use the model: notice your default circle first

Coaching sources that use the model give the same first exercise. Notice which circle you fall into when you are tired, surprised, or attacked. This is your default circle. Your default circle is the circle that you return to without a decision. Rodenburg's book gives physical exercises to move from the default circle to the second circle: a body checklist, breathing exercises, and exercises with one object. The teaching plan of BYU (source 4) walks through the body checklist.

4. Why leaders hear about presence: 268 executives put it at 26% of promotion

The word "presence" reaches engineers through a second route: the business term executive presence. Executive presence is the impression that you are leadership material, as judged by senior people. The economist Sylvia Ann Hewlett measured how much this impression counts.

Guess first

Hewlett's team asked 268 senior executives what share of "what it takes to get promoted" is executive presence. Choose a number before you continue: about 10%, about 25%, or about 50%.

Answer to the guess: the 268 executives put executive presence at 26% of what it takes to get promoted into a leadership position. Of the same 268 executives, 67% (about 180 of 268) named gravitas as the most important part of executive presence. Gravitas is explained in the section "Words that you will hear". These numbers come from a survey, so they measure opinion, not cause. The label is Medium, because I read them in a business-school summary and not in the original report.

The link to Rodenburg's model is this. Hewlett's survey shows that senior people judge a leader by an impression. Rodenburg's model gives one concrete, physical description of the impression: the second circle. The survey does not test Rodenburg's model, and Rodenburg's book does not use the survey. The two sources come from different fields, and this page puts them side by side.

5. Wrong idea corrected: more outward energy is not more presence

Common wrong idea

Wrong: "Presence means a louder voice, bigger gestures, and visible confidence. To have more presence, push more energy outward."

Correct: pushed outward energy is the third circle. Rodenburg says of the third circle: "it's an energy that takes the oxygen out of the room". The Roffey Park summary of her model says: "you cannot be present in this place". Presence is the second circle, where the energy goes to one person and comes back.

Why the wrong idea fails: the third circle sends the energy past the target. The people in the room are not addressed as persons, so nobody feels heard. A person who feels pushed cannot exchange energy with you, and the exchange is the definition of presence. The wrong idea also has a second cost. A person in the third circle "cannot listen", so the leader loses the information that the team has.

6. Limits: a practitioner model, not a tested theory

Limits
  • No controlled study. The model comes from 40 years of work with actors. On 30 September 2026 I searched for experiments that test the three circles, and I found none. The claims about what others feel are the observations of coaches, not measurements.
  • Secondary sources. I read Rodenburg's definitions in three coaching summaries and in a teaching plan that quotes pages 45 to 49 of the book. I did not read the full book. A definition on this page can differ in detail from the book.
  • States, not types. The circles describe a state that lasts seconds or minutes. They are not personality types. A quiet person can be in the second circle, and a loud person can be in the first circle.
  • The model covers the how, not the what. The second circle does not make a bad decision good. It only makes the decision heard and lets the team answer.
  • Eye contact differs between cultures. The sign "eyes on one person" in the table is Rodenburg's European and American context. In some cultures, long direct eye contact with a senior person is impolite. The direction of attention is the point, not the eyes.

7. Words that you will hear

These terms appear in leadership trainings and in feedback about presence. They are not part of Rodenburg's model, but you will meet them next to it.

Executive presence
The impression, judged by senior people, that you are leadership material. Hewlett's survey divides it into gravitas, communication, and appearance. Relation to the main idea: Rodenburg's second circle is one physical way to produce this impression.
Gravitas
The impression of seriousness, calm, and good judgment under pressure. Relation to the main idea: a leader in the second circle stays calm and listens, which observers read as gravitas.
Charisma
A personal quality that attracts and moves other people. Relation to the main idea: coaches warn that charisma is often third-circle energy, which impresses but does not connect.
Active listening
A listening technique: you show attention, you repeat the main point of the speaker, and you ask questions. Relation to the main idea: active listening is what the second circle looks like during the listening half of the exchange.
Grounding, or centring
Exercises that bring attention to the feet, the breath, and the balance of the body. Relation to the main idea: Rodenburg's body checklist is a grounding exercise. Its goal is a second-circle body.
Authenticity
The impression that a leader shows who they really are. Relation to the main idea: one coaching source ties the two directly. It says: "It is virtually impossible to be an authentic leader unless you are in the second circle."

8. Check your understanding

Try to answer before you open each answer.
  1. A manager stands up in a meeting, speaks loudly, scans the room, and continues without a pause. A colleague says: "That manager has real presence." Which circle is the manager in, according to Rodenburg, and is the colleague right?
    Show the answer

    Third circle. The colleague is wrong in Rodenburg's terms.

    Loud voice, eyes on nobody in particular, and no pause are the signs of outward, general energy. Rodenburg calls this the third circle, and she says that you cannot be present there. The typical wrong answer is "second circle, because the manager is confident". Confidence is not the test. The test is: does the energy reach one person and come back? See section 5.

  2. You must tell one engineer that their design was rejected. Name three physical things that you do before the first sentence, and give the reason for each.
    Show the answer

    Any three of these, from the worked example in section 1:

    1. Feet flat on the floor and shoulders free, because the body is the first place where the circle shows.
    2. One slow breath out, because a pushed breath is the third circle and a shallow breath is the first circle.
    3. Eyes on the engineer, because the second circle needs one specific target.
    4. A pause after the fact, because the energy must come back before you send more.
  3. A database goes down during the meeting. You say loudly to the room: "Everyone stop all deployments now." Is this a mistake?
    Show the answer

    No, if it stays short.

    Rodenburg allows the third circle "to respond to a crisis". One general, forceful instruction is the correct use. The mistake begins if you stay in the third circle after the instruction, because then nobody can tell you what they see in the logs. Return to the second circle: look at the engineer who is closest to the database and ask what they see. See section 3.

  4. Hewlett's survey asked 268 senior executives about promotion. What share of "what it takes to get promoted" did they give to executive presence? What does this number not show?
    Show the answer

    26%. It does not show cause.

    The number is the opinion of the executives about the share of promotion that presence explains. It is not a measurement of promotions. It also does not test Rodenburg's model. The typical wrong answer mixes this number with the 67% for gravitas, which is a different question. See section 4.

9. Recap, glossary, and sources

Recap
  1. Rodenburg's energy is attention, breath, and voice, directed somewhere. The circle is the direction.
  2. First circle: inward. Second circle: to one person and back. Third circle: outward to everyone, with force.
  3. Presence is the second circle. Louder and bigger is the third circle, and it is not presence.
  4. For a leader: before you speak or listen, put the feet on the floor, breathe out, look at one person, say one sentence, and wait.
  5. The model is a practitioner model from theatre. No controlled study tests it. A survey of 268 executives puts executive presence at 26% of promotion, but that survey is about the impression, not about the model.

Glossary

Default circle
The circle that you return to without a decision, for example when you are tired or attacked.
Energy
In Rodenburg's model: your attention, breath, and voice, taken together and directed somewhere. Not a physical quantity.
Executive presence
The impression, judged by senior people, that you are leadership material.
First circle
The circle of withdrawal. The energy goes inward, to yourself.
Presence
The state where your attention is on the people in front of you, and they can feel it. In Rodenburg's model, presence is the second circle.
Second circle
The circle of connection. The energy goes to one specific person or object and comes back.
Third circle
The circle of force. The energy goes outward to nobody in particular, or at a target with force, with the motive of control.
